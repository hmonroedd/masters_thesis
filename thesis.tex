% Your abstract should cover at least the following topics, 1-2 sentences for

Deuterium-tritium fusion power plants must breed their own tritium, and recover it fast enough to mitigate permeation to structural materials and keep the fuel cycle closed. 
% In molten salt breeders the recovery step is poorly constrained: the transport properties of tritium in the salt are known only to within orders of magnitude, and large uncertainties remain regarding tritium behavior once bred into the salt. 
This work addresses gas sparging as an in-situ tritium extraction system for breeding experiments in molten salts. An inert gas is bubbled through the breeder so that tritium transfers to the rising bubbles and leaves with them. No cheap model predicts how quickly a gas sparging extraction system will strip the salt, it is the purpose of this work to develop one: a one-dimensional advection-reaction-dispersion model of the salt and gas phases is used, closed by user specified algebraic relations that are resolved automatically, the whole is released as an open-source package. Solving it analytically under four explicit assumptions yields a closed form for the extraction time, $\tau=\frac{\langle\varepsilon_l\rangle}{\langle a\,h_l\rangle}\cdot\PI/(1-e^{-\PI})$, governed by a single dimensionless saturation number $\langle\Pi\rangle$ that measures how close a bubble comes to equilibrium with the liquid before it leaves. A 300-sample sweep of the design space, spanning the three dimensionless groups that carry the assumptions, shows that this closed form reproduces the numerical model to within $\pm5\%$ whenever at most one assumption is violated, and fails by up to $46\%$ as soon as two are; on top of temperature and gas flow rate, it also identifies pressure as a significant design lever, improving the extraction time up to a factor 8 when pulling vacuum in the head space. Applied to the forthcoming LIBRA Pi experiment and its 100 L of ClLiF, the model predicts a tritium removal time of \textbf{2.3 to 5.3 days} at nominal operating conditions, the spread reflecting the uncertainty on the salt transport properties rather than on the operating point, with temperature and gas flow rate carrying almost all of the sensitivity. Coupling the extraction time to a three-dimensional simulation of tritium permeation through the double-walled vessel of LIBRA Pi shows that permeation competes with sparging rather than being negligible against it, and that the composition of the sweep gas between the two walls decides whether the permeated tritium is collected or lost: with helium alone up to $12\%$ of the bred tritium can escape to the environment, this goes down to less than $0.4\%$ once $3.5\%$ of \ce{H2} is added.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation: fusion energy}
% what is nuclear fusion
Nuclear fusion is the process in which 2 light atom nuclei (reactant) combine together to form 1 heavier nucleus (product). The mass difference $\Delta m$ between reactant and product is converted into energy $E=\Delta m \, c^2$. Fusion reactions happen at the core of our stars and the energy they release is what gives us daylight and enables any life on earth. Artificial fusion reactions were first achieved by humans in the 1930s, at that time research in nuclear fusion was mainly motivated by military purposes. But just like nuclear fission found a peaceful use in nuclear reactors for energy production, nuclear fusion can be used as a carbon-free and virtually inexhaustible energy source.

% where are we with fusion
It requires extreme temperature and pressure conditions comparable to that of our Sun for fusion reactions to happen. This makes controlled fusion a problem of unprecedented complexity, with challenges still to be overcome before a commercially viable fusion power plant (FPP) becomes possible. Conventional nuclear reactors on the other hand, which are based on the reverse process of nuclear fission where a heavy nucleus splits into 2 lighter nuclei, have been providing electricity to the grid for more than 70 years. Nevertheless, compared to well-proven fission power plants, the fusion approach would present the advantage of lower and shorter radiotoxicity of its byproducts, increased safety and abundance of fuel. As humanity is facing the need of reducing its carbon emission while maintaining its energy supply, fusion power is increasingly seen as a promising candidate to fulfill this need: in 2026, the Fusion Industry Association numbered 56 private fusion companies for a total funding of \$14.2bn worldwide, which is \$4.5bn more than in 2025\cite{noauthor_2026_2026}. 

% introducing tritium
Among these 56 companies, 40 of them envision deuterium-tritium (D-T) as reactants of the fusion reactions. Indeed, the D-T couple offers a favorable cross section for fusion reactions as evidenced by \cref{fig:cross_sections}, i.e it is the fuel choice that maximizes the likeliness of fusion reactions to happen at the conditions that can be achieved with the currently available technology. 

Deuterium is present in small proportions in seawater and its extraction is a well known process: it is used as a neutron moderator in the Pressurized Heavy Water Reactors (PHWR) operating worldwide. Tritium fuel supply on the other hand faces a fundamental scarcity problem.

\section{The tritium isotope}
% what is tritium (main characteristics)
Tritium is a radioactive isotope of hydrogen, its nucleus is made of 1 proton and 2 neutrons and is therefore denoted \ce{^3H} or \ce{T}. It disintegrates into \ce{^3He} through beta decay with a half life of 12.3 years. 
% where do we find it
Because of its short half life and its absence of any decay chain, tritium can't be naturally found in concentrated quantities unlike other radioactive isotopes (e.g. uranium-238, radon-222), and is therefore extremely scarce. It is unintentionally produced: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item naturally: from highly energetic cosmic neutrons interacting with atmospheric nitrogen (\ce{^14_7N(n,T)^12_6C} reaction, contribution estimated at 0.2 kg of T/year)
    \item artificially: in all fission reactors, tritium is a low yield product of fission reactions ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$ T/fission event\cite{noauthor_livechart_nodate}). In that context, it is an undesirable product due to its radioactivity: tritium can substitute to hydrogen in water, which if spread in the environment can contaminate the food chain and become a radiological hazard once ingested. %https://www-nds.iaea.org/relnsd/vcharthtml/VChartHTML.html
\end{itemize}
Both these tritium sources are too marginal to make a technically/economically feasible tritium stream. 


\section{Tritium production}
\subsection{Main pathways}
% how can we create tritium 
A typical FPP would require important amounts of tritium, estimated at $\sim$55 kg/year per GW of fusion power \cite{abdou_physics_2020}. The current world inventory of $\sim$20 kg of tritium is largely insufficient and halves every 12 years due to radioactive decay if not renewed. It is then necessary to intentionally produce tritium via nuclear transmutation:
\begin{align}
%include reaction with B10 ? -> in PWR 
    \ce{^2H + n &-> T + \gamma} \label{eq:D2_capture}\\
    \ce{^6Li + n &-> T + \alpha} + 4.8~\mathrm{MeV} \label{eq:Li6_capture}\\
    \ce{^7Li + n &-> T + \alpha} + \mathrm{n} - 2.5~\mathrm{MeV} \label{eq:Li7_capture}
\end{align}

As of now, the existing commercially available tritium is produced as a byproduct of heavy water moderated reactors through neutron capture (\cref{eq:D2_capture}). The contribution of CANada Deuterium Uranium (CANDU) reactors specifically is estimated at 130 g of tritium/GWe/year from \ce{D(n,\gamma)T} reaction in heavy water\cite{abdou_physics_2020}. But the small cross section of this reaction (0.5 millibarns for thermal neutrons) makes it unsuitable for large scale production. \Cref{eq:Li6_capture} on the other hand  has a 1000~barns thermal neutron cross section\cite{noauthor_janis_nodate}, it is used for military tritium production and is also the privileged path to tritium breeding for controlled fusion. Industrial tritium production would therefore rely on thermal neutron capture in a lithium-6 rich material, the (n,T) reaction of \ce{^7Li} being endothermic, its cross section becomes significant only in the fast region of the neutron spectrum.

Lithium itself is abundant, but the isotope that matters is not. Natural lithium contains only $7.5\%$ of \ce{^6Li}, the isotope with the large thermal capture cross section, the rest being \ce{^7Li} whose own tritium-producing reaction is endothermic and a hundred times less likely. A breeding blanket therefore relies on isotopic \ce{^6Li} enrichment up to 90\%\cite{forsberg_fusion_2020}, which is an industrial process performed by very few actors. On the other hand, tritium is an unwanted byproduct in advanced fission reactor based on molten salts, requiring the use of \ce{^6Li} depleted or \ce{^7Li} enriched salts up to 99.99\% \ce{^7Li}\cite{dolan_experimental_2023}. This emphasizes the need for isotopic separation facilities, from  which  both the fusion and fission industries would benefit\cite{forsberg_fusion_2020}.

% where do we get the neutrons from
Aforementioned transmutation reactions all need neutrons as a reactant. Neutrons can be found in fission reactors, but producing tritium there poses the problem of introducing large amounts of tritium in an environment where it is usually unwanted because of its handling difficulties and the associated radiological concern presented above. Tritium production in fission reactors is anyway limited to relatively low amounts ($\sim$1 kg/reactor/year\cite{abdou_physics_2020}), as transmutation reactions consume neutrons that are also needed for fission to self-sustain. For those reasons, tritium production in fission reactors is restricted to military programs.

DT fusion itself produces fast neutrons (\cref{eq:DT_fusion}), it naturally follows that a FPP can act as a neutron source that \textit{breeds} its own fuel. Contrary to external sources such as fission reactors or neutron beams, this approach offers the advantage that the available neutron flux scales with the amount of fuel needed and thus can sustain operation of an arbitrarily large fleet of FPP. This makes breeding the most scalable and economical path to industrial tritium fuel production. 

\begin{align}
    \ce{D + T -> \alpha}\ \mathrm{(3.5 MeV) + n \ (14 MeV)} \label{eq:DT_fusion}
\end{align}
    

\subsection{The breeding blanket}
% how is breeding engineered ?
In practice, a FPP would produce tritium in a \textit{breeding blanket} that surrounds the fusion core, be it inertially or magnetically confined. For \cref{eq:Li6_capture,eq:Li7_capture} to occur, the breeding blanket is made of a lithium-containing material, chosen among:
\begin{itemize}
    \item solid ceramics: lithium orthosilicate (\ce{Li4SiO4}), lithium metatitanate (\ce{Li2TiO3})
    \item liquid metals: Lithium-Lead eutectic (\ce{Pb_{84}Li_{16}}), melting at 235°C
    \item molten salts: FLiBe (\ce{2LiF-BeF2}), FLiNaK (\ce{LiF-NaF-KF}), melting at $\sim$450°C
\end{itemize}

% material choice: tritium production
From the perspective of tritium production optimization, the choice of breeding material is driven by: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item a high macroscopic neutron capture cross section: higher Li density, \ce{ ^6Li} enrichment is favorable
    \item presence of a neutron moderator: the \ce{^6Li} reaction occurs with thermal neutrons, therefore light elements are favorable for their moderating capability
    \item presence of a neutron multiplier: Pb and Be containing materials readily incorporate a neutron multiplier ((n,2n) reaction)
\end{itemize}

% the other roles of the BB
Beyond the tritium production aspect, a breeding blanket must shield the 14 MeV neutrons produced by the fusion core, i.e. 80\% of the fusion energy (see \cref{eq:DT_fusion}) dissipates in it as heat. In that regard, liquid materials offer the advantage of being both tritium breeding materials and coolant. Molten salts in particular have a heat capacity superior to that of LiPb and don't conduct electricity which makes them favorable for compact and magnetically confined FPP designs, such as the Affordable-Robust-Compact (ARC) tokamak concept\cite{sorbom_arc_2015}. 

Among liquid breeder concepts, the liquid immersion blanket takes the idea to its limit: rather than circulating the breeder through channels inside a structured blanket, it immerses the entire fusion core in a large volume of molten salt. The advantages of this design is its simplicity: there is no complex blanket structure to be qualified under neutron irradiation, the same fluid breeds, shields and cools, and the solid angle covered around the core approaches unity, which directly raises the achievable breeding ratio\cite{ferrero_multiphysics_2025}. The cost is a very large tritium inventory dissolved in a very large volume of salt, which is precisely what makes both its extraction and its accountancy difficult\cite{ferry_libra_2023}.


\section{Challenges of the tritium fuel cycle}
The fusion fuel cycle is the set of processes that supplies tritium to the plasma, divided in two independent closed loops: % "independent": outer loop doesn't have the output of inner loop as input, contrary to what the "inner" and "outer" denominations suggest
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{inner fuel cycle}: recovery of what the plasma does not burn. It is characterized by the fueling efficiency (performance of the fuel injection system, i.e. how much fuel reaches the fusion core) and the tritium burn fraction TBF (performance of the fusion core, i.e. how much tritium fuses among the one that reached the core). The product of these two quantities isn't expected to exceed 5\% \cite{abdou_physics_2020}
    \item \textit{outer fuel cycle}: newly bred tritium to replace what was consumed. It is characterized by the Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR), defined as the ratio of tritium bred per tritium consumed. 
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Self-sufficiency}
% definition of the scientific and engineering TBR, tritium self-sufficiency of a FPP
% include doubling time considerations, achievable and required TBR ? (Ferry 2023, Abdou 2020)

For the fusion power industry to be able to scale, individual FPPs have to produces at least as much tritium as they consumes, i.e. optimize their outer fuel cycle to achieve a sufficiently high TBR. For explanatory purposes. a distinction can be made between the "neutronics" TBR (\cref{eq:TBR_neutronics}), which measures tritium being born in the breeding material from transmutation reactions, and the "real" TBR (\cref{eq:TBR_real}) which measures the tritium that is actually retrieved and can be injected in the fusion core. 
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}}&= \frac{\mathrm{T_{bred}}}{\mathrm{T_{burnt}}} \label{eq:TBR_neutronics} \\
    \mathrm{TBR_{real}} &= \frac{\mathrm{T_{retrieved}}}{\mathrm{T_{burnt}}} =  \mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}} \cdot \eta  \label{eq:TBR_real}\\
    \eta &= \frac{\mathrm{T_{retrieved}}}{\mathrm{T_{bred}}}<1 \label{eq:eta}
\end{align}

Where $\eta$ accounts for losses in the outer fuel cycle. 

Common breeding blanket designs manage to achieve $\mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}}\approx1.1$\cite{forsberg_fusion_2020}. Nevertheless, $\mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}}$>1 is necessary but not sufficient. A self sufficient FPP needs to achieve $\mathrm{TBR_{real}}$>1 by maximizing both its components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}}$ is purely neutronics dependent, it is optimized by use of \ce{^6Li} enrichment and by maximizing the breeding blanket's thickness as well as the solid angle it covers around the fusion core;
    \item $\eta$ is optimized by reducing permeation losses throughout the fuel cycle (e.g. by means of dedicated structural materials and coatings) and optimizing tritium extraction from the breeder.
\end{itemize}  

\subsection{Tritium permeation}
% tritium is very mobile
As isotopes of the same element share same electron structure, tritium chemically behaves like hydrogen (notwithstanding a few nuances in their kinetic properties arising from their mass difference). Tritium forms \ce{T2} gas and can substitutes with hydrogen to form tritiated water \ce{HTO}. It can dissolve as atomic \ce{T} in metallic materials, as a consequence of hydrogen being a small but non-inert element, enabling a 3-step process called \textit{permeation}: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item tritium bonds with the conduction band electrons at the metal surface (dissociation of \ce{T2} into adsorbed \ce{T})
    \item it then fits into interstitial sites of the lattice (high \textit{solubility} in metals) and hops between them over a low energy barrier (high \textit{diffusivity})
    \item  until it reaches the opposite free surface where it becomes \ce{T2} gas again (recombination and desorption)
\end{enumerate}
Permeation is characterized by \textit{permeability} = diffusivity $\times$ solubility, and makes tritium especially hard to contain no matter how leak-tight and defect-free the container is. 

Permeation represents a significant part of outer fuel cycle losses (i.e. degrades $\eta$) and can pose a radiological issue. This is especially the case in liquid breeders that need to operate above a certain melting point, since permeability increases with temperature. 

\subsection{Tritium extraction}
On top of directly retrieving tritium, maximizing the efficiency of the tritium extraction system (TES) has the indirect positive effect of reducing the amount of tritium available for permeation, therefore decreasing tritium permeation losses in the outer fuel cycle. A performant TES is therefore needed to achieve a sufficiently high $\eta$. 

The existing TES technologies for liquid breeders are \cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vacuum permeators}: the tritium-bearing liquid flows past a membrane, usually a thin metal wall, on the other side of which a vacuum is maintained. Tritium permeates through the membrane and is pumped away. The technology is passive and produces a pure tritium stream, but it relies on a large membrane area and on that membrane staying free of the oxide layers that would throttle it.
    \item \textbf{Gas-liquid contactors}: an inert gas is brought into contact with the liquid over as large an interfacial area as possible, and carries the tritium away. Bubble columns disperse the gas in the liquid, packed columns disperse the liquid over a packing. They are mechanically simple and well documented in the chemical engineering literature, at the cost of diluting the tritium in a large gas stream. Gas sparging, the object of this work, belongs to this family.
    \item \textbf{Vacuum sieve trays}: the liquid is broken into a rain of droplets falling through a vacuum, so that the tritium escapes from a surface that is renewed continuously. The efficiency per pass is high, but the technology requires handling a free-falling liquid metal or salt under vacuum.
\end{itemize}

\section{Existing gaps}

Both inner and outer fuel cycle are the subject of intense research, with the aim not to lose tritium and maximize its production. 

On the outer fuel cycle side, the main focus is to better understand tritium handling and extraction, in other words to be able to predict and maximize $\mathrm{TBR_{real}}$. In the context of the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), the OFC aspect of ceramic and liquid metal based breeders was investigated in several studies\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011,utili_tritium_2019,abdou_physics_2020}.

There is however large uncertainties when it comes to molten salt based breeders, due to the challenges involved in experimenting with them: high temperatures, corrosion, Be toxicity and general tritium handling. This gap is addressed by the Liquid Immersion Blanket: Robust Accountancy (LIBRA) experiment, which main goal is to demonstrate accurate prediction of tritium breeding (the $\mathrm{TBR_{neutronics}}$ component) and extraction (the $\eta$ component), in a FLiBe salt breeder and DT fusion conditions (14 MeV neutrons)\cite{ferry_libra_2023}. 

Early results from this program are encouraging. The BABY 1L campaign, conducted on litre-scale salt volumes, showed good tritium recovery without any dedicated extraction system: tritium was collected simply by letting it diffuse through the salt to the free surface, or permeate through the container walls \cite{delaporte-mathurin_baby_2026}. At this scale, passive transport is sufficient.

This approach does not survive scaling, as the characteristic time for tritium to diffuse out of the salt grows quadratically with the salt dimensions. The forthcoming LIBRA Pi experiment will operate on a salt volume of $\sim$100\,L, its characteristic diffusion time is $\frac{L^2}{D}=\frac{(30\ \mathrm{cm})^2}{5\cdot10^{-9} \ \mathrm{m^2/s}}\approx200 \ \mathrm{days}$ if no convective transport is considered. Such a timescale is incompatible with the experimental iteration rate the program requires, and an active extraction system therefore becomes necessary.

Gas sparging was identified as a candidate to fill this role. The principle is to bubble an inert gas (typically helium) through the liquid breeder: tritium transfers from the liquid to the rising bubbles across the gas-liquid interface and is carried out of the tank, while the motion of the bubbles simultaneously promotes advective tritium transport within the liquid itself. The process is affordable, mechanically simple, and well documented in the chemical engineering literature \cite{deckwer_mixing_1974}.

% roadmap: 1 sentence/chapter

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{What extraction timescales could a sparging system achieve in LIBRA Pi ?}
    \item \textit{What are the mechanisms affecting extraction kinetics of gas sparging and how can it be optimized ?} 
    \item \textit{What are the limitations of this prediction and how can it be improved ?}
\end{itemize}

Tritium transport models are built from two fundamental properties: diffusivity and solubility. It was established that the gases formed by hydrogen isotopes are only weakly soluble in fluoride salts, leading them to permeate in materials where they are more soluble like metals; the same study observed that the saturation pressure has a linear dependence on the tritium concentration in the salt, indicating a Henry's law behavior $c=K_s\,P$\cite{malinauskas_solubilities_1974}. 

Tritium transport properties are speciation dependent: \textit{ab initio} molecular dynamics calculations suggest that tritium might diffuse 3 to 5 times slower when present as \ce{TF} rather than \ce{T2}\cite{lam_impact_2021}. The \ce{TF}/\ce{T2} branching depends on redox control, usually performed using sacrificial \ce{Be} metal as reducing agent\cite{petti_jupiter-ii_2006}: the affinity of the highly electronegative \ce{F^-} ions for \ce{Be^{2+}} drives the redox potential toward formation of \ce{BeF2} and \ce{T2}/\ce{HT} rather than letting free \ce{T^+} form \ce{TF}. 

This makes tritium transport measurements hard to reproduce, leading to large discrepancies in the measured transport properties depending on the experimental apparatus and redox control behind each measurements\cite{anderl_deuteriumtritium_2004,saraswat_permeation_2026}. Discrepancies are amplified by the fact that the transport properties are measured for a liquid that must be enclosed in a container: tritium first has to permeate through the structural metal and potentially some Ni coating, which adds some experimental variability due to the uncertain kinetic of the reactions happening at the salt-metal interface\cite{fukada_diffusion_2005}. A comparison of the results across studies was compiled by \textcite{humrickhouse_tritium_2020} and replicated in \cref{fig:transport_params}, showing variations of the Henry's constant by up to 2 orders of magnitude. 

The modeling carried on in this work therefore builds on uncertain foundations, the large uncertainties on tritium transport properties will be dealt with by considering various correlations and propagating the impact of the correlation choice to the model's output. 

Physical properties of fluoride salts FLiBe and FLiNaK on the other hand are comparatively well known. The study of fluoride salts has been motivated by both fusion and fission applications, as fluoride salts can be used as coolant in advanced reactor concepts such as Fluoride-salt-cooled High-temperature Reactors (FHR)\cite{stempien_integrated_2016}. First measurement of FLiBe properties such as density, viscosity and surface tension were performed in the 1960s in the context of the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE)\cite{cantor_physical_1968} and enriched since then \cite{romatoski_fluoride_2017,vidrio_density_2022}. ClLiF salt on the other hand, which is used in the BABY 1L experiments\cite{delaporte-mathurin_baby_2026} and is envisioned for the LIBRA Pi experiment, was disregarded by fission and fusion research until now, its tritium transport properties are unmeasured, and its physical properties far less characterized
than those of FLiBe. 

\section{Gas sparging in molten salts}

The MSRE pioneered the use of He sparging in FLiBe salt. Intended for removal of the fission product Xe-135 from the salt that embedded the fission fuel, the sparging system turned out to also remove tritium from the reactor, with a contribution estimated at \textbf{58 to 65\% of the tritium of the reactor carried out through the fuel salt off-gas system}\cite{briggs_calculation_1970}. The experimental data of the MSRE also established a correlation for tritium mass transport from salt to inert gas bubbles, which will be used in this work. The gas fraction was deduced from the effect of bubbles' void on reactivity, and a fraction of 1\% was sufficient to strip 98\% of the Xe-135\cite{evans_physics_2026}. Still in the MSRE, tritium diffusion in atomic form through metal walls was recognized as a significant leakage pathway\cite{robertson_conceptual_1971}, and 12 to 25\% of the tritium was left unaccounted and assumed to be permanently retained in the system\cite{briggs_calculation_1970}. This motivates recent studies on tritium transport and accountancy. 

The Molten Salt Breeder Reactor (MSBR) was planned to succeed the MSRE with further experiments to better characterize the mass transfer of Xe from molten salt to inert gas bubbles\cite{peebles_removal_1968}, but the project got aborted. Today's molten salt reactor research builds on the MSRE and MSBR heritage, it constitutes an extended literature for transport of isotopes in molten salts: \textcite{taylor_implementation_2022} implemented a 3D two-phase model for Xe transport in molten salt reactors; \textcite{yang_multiphase_2025} developed a 1D model for transport of noble metals fission products in molten salt reactors; and \textcite{evans_physics_2026} recently proposed a thorough review of gas sparging in MSR for removal of noble metals and tritium specifically. 

\section{Gas-liquid contactors for tritium extraction}

Extracting tritium from a liquid breeder is not specific to molten salts. The question was studied at length for the lithium-lead eutectic foreseen in the ITER and DEMO breeding blankets, where the candidate technologies were narrowed down to gas-liquid contactors, permeators and vacuum sieve trays\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}. Gas-liquid contactors cover both bubble columns, where the gas is dispersed in the liquid, and packed columns, where the liquid flows over a packing structure that maximizes the contact area: the first were modelled and the second tested experimentally on Pb-17Li\cite{malara_tritium_1995,alpy_hydrogen_1998}, and complete extraction loops were later designed around them\cite{mohan_experimental_2010}. Bubble columns are the simplest of these to build and to operate, at the cost of a lower interfacial area, since bubbles coalesce as they rise, and of a strong back-mixing of the liquid that reduces the driving force for the transfer\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}.

The model developed in this work descends directly from this line of work. \textcite{malara_tritium_1995} described a bubble column extracting tritium from Pb-17Li with a one-dimensional convection-dispersion model closed by Danckwerts boundary conditions, which is the formulation adapted to molten salts in \cref{sec:sparging_maths}. What changes from the lithium-lead case is the closure: the hydrodynamics and the interfacial transfer must be described by correlations valid for a molten salt, and it is there that the uncertainty discussed in \cref{sec:background} enters the model.

One feature of the lithium-lead work carries over and is worth keeping in mind: doping the stripping gas with \ce{H2} increases the net tritium flux at the interface, because isotopic exchange lets the liquid give up tritium while absorbing protium\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}. The same mechanism reappears in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_permeation}, this time on the metal side, where the composition of the sweep gas decides whether the permeated tritium is collected or lost.

Because hydrodynamic measurements in molten salts are scarce, the closure relations used here are borrowed from the chemical engineering literature on bubble columns, which is mature and largely empirical. Interfacial transfer is described by the two-film model of \textcite{lewis_principles_1924} and the transport along the column by the axial dispersion model popularized by Levenspiel, both of which are bases for the standard models of bubble column reactors\cite{levenspiel_chemical_1999,schluter_modeling_1992}. The individual quantities they require, namely the interfacial area, the mass transfer coefficient and the axial dispersion coefficients, can be obtained by correlations fitted on air-water and on viscous organic systems \cite{calderbank_continuous_1961,deckwer_mixing_1974,bhavaraju_design_1978,hebrard_characterization_1999}. Applying them to a molten fluoride salt is an extrapolation, and one that this work does not attempt to justify beyond checking that the resulting dimensionless groups stay within the range where the correlations were established.


\section{State of modelling}

Tritium transport in fluoride salts has been modelled at every scale. At the component scale, \textcite{humrickhouse_tritium_2020} reviewed the transport phenomena and the correlations available for them, and \textcite{stempien_integrated_2016} coupled transport and corrosion in an integrated model of a fluoride-salt-cooled reactor. At the plant scale, tritium control is treated as a system problem in which extraction competes with permeation and retention for the same inventory\cite{forsberg_tritium_2017,christensen_tritium_2018}, a competition that this work quantifies for LIBRA Pi in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_permeation}.

Two families of models frame the present work, and they sit at opposite ends of the cost spectrum. Fuel cycle models describe the whole plant with lumped compartments, and a tritium extraction system enters them as a single time constant to be supplied from outside\cite{meschini_modeling_2023,delaporte-mathurin_physics-informed_2026}. At the other end, \textcite{li_toward_2026} recently simulated gas-liquid contactors in molten salts in three dimensions with a volume of fluid method, resolving individual bubble interfaces. Such a simulation describes the interface far better than any correlation, but its cost forbids the hundreds of runs needed to explore a design space.

Between a lumped model that takes the extraction time as an input and a three-dimensional CFD simulation that resolves single bubbles, there is no cheap model that predicts the extraction time of a sparging system in a molten salt from its design and operating parameters. Providing one is the object of \cref{sec:sparging_model}: the one-dimensional formulation is inherited from the Li-Pb work described above, the novelty lies in closing it with molten salt correlations resolved dynamically from the user specifications, and in mapping where the resulting closed-form solution can be trusted. The low cost of this approach makes the design space study of \cref{sec:sparging_expl} and the sensitivity analysis of \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging} possible.

\section{The LIBRA programme}

The compact high-field tokamaks that renewed interest in molten salt breeders, ARC among them\cite{sorbom_arc_2015}, would immerse the fusion core in a large volume of liquid salt, so that the same fluid breeds the tritium, shields the neutrons and removes the heat. High flux irradiation of FLiBe salt with thermal neutrons and subsequent observation of tritium behavior was already performed in the context of FHR research\cite{dolan_experimental_2023}. However, precise accountancy of tritium bred from 14 MeV neutrons in fluoride salts was never demonstrated. Gaining a complete understanding of tritium breeding, accountancy and recovery from such a blanket material is the goal of the LIBRA programme\cite{ferry_libra_2023}, which approaches it by scaling up: the BABY experiments bred and collected tritium in litre-scale salt volumes, and reported that more than 96\% of it was collected as \ce{T2} and \ce{HT}\cite{delaporte-mathurin_baby_2026}. That result is what allows the present work to model a single chemical species, as stated in \cref{sec:sparging_assumptions}.

LIBRA Pi scales the same experiment to roughly 100 litres, and the constraints it operates under are what the model of this work has to take as inputs. The salt is ClLiF rather than FLiBe, chosen to avoid beryllium toxicity at the cost of unknown transport properties; the temperature is bounded by the available heaters; the sparging gas flow rate is bounded by the water bubblers that strip the tritium from it downstream; and the tritium inventory available for extraction is set by a 14 MeV neutron generator. These bounds define the operating window explored in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging}.

\section{Mathematical framework}
\label{sec:sparging_maths}

\subsection{Definitions}
\label{sec:generic_definitions}

Let us first introduce some concepts that will be useful in the rest of this work.

The system to be described is made of a liquid ($l$, the molten salt) and a gas phase ($g$, inert gas bubbles), enclosed in a tank ($t$) that delimits the domain. The concentration field in each phase $\alpha$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    c^\dag_\alpha(\vec r,t)\equiv \left \{ \begin{array}{rcl}
         &  \frac{d n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}(\vec r,t)}{d V_\alpha(\vec r)} , & \vec r\in\alpha\\
         &  0 & \vec r \notin \alpha
    \end{array}\right.
\end{equation}

This corresponds to a phase specific or so-called \textit{intrinsic} quantity of the phase (as defined by \cite{whitaker_method_1999}), in the fact that it represents the accumulation of a species \textit{per unit volume of the phase} ($d V_\alpha$) and not \textit{per unit volume of the porous medium} ($d V_t$). Due to the inherently discontinuous nature of two-fluid systems, such concentrations field is defined and continuous only in its respective phase.

Such definition is physically intuitive, it doesn't hide the discontinuous nature of the system and is therefore conceptually useful to understand the model presented hereafter. However, working with a discontinuous function is inconvenient. We can use the spatial smoothing methodology presented in \cite{gray_derivation_1975} and define an \textit{averaging volume} denoted $\delta V_t$. It can be seen as a mesoscale volume: large compared to bubbles/pores (microscale, $dV_t$) but small compared to the tank (macroscale, $V_t$). Let us then extend the definition of $c^\dag_\alpha$ to a field $c_\alpha$ that is defined and uniform in the whole averaging volume, and introduce the concept of phase fraction of $\alpha$:

% In this two-fluid system, the concentration fields of the liquid and gas phases (respectively $\CT \ \mathrm{[molT_2/m^3]}$ and $\PT \ \mathrm{[Pa]}$) are defined 
\begin{align}
    % \CT(z,t) &\equiv \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},l}(z,t)}{\delta V_l(z)} \label{eq:def_CT} \\
    % \PT(z,t) &\equiv \frac{1}{RT} \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},g}(z,t)}{\delta V_g(z)} \label{eq:def_PT}\\
    c_\alpha (\vec r,t) &\equiv \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}(\vec r,t)}{\delta V_\alpha(\vec r)} \label{eq:def_c_generic}\\
    \varepsilon_\alpha(\vec r) &\equiv \frac{\delta V_\alpha(\vec r)}{\delta V_t(\vec r)} \label{eq:def_eps}
\end{align}

Where $\delta V_\alpha$ is the volume of phase $\alpha$ contained in $\delta V_t$ (the averaging volume), and $\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}$ is the amount of \ce{T_2} in $\delta V_\alpha$. The field $c_\alpha$ is continuous everywhere in the domain, but because it was artificially extended we can't integrate it as such over the whole domain: this would overestimate the amount of species present in phase $\alpha$, because we would account for a non-zero concentration in regions of the domain where it was actually zero in the discontinuous definition $c^\dag_\alpha$. $\varepsilon_\alpha$ ensure that the total quantity of species $n_\alpha\equiv\iiint_{V_t}dn_\alpha$ is conserved between the continuous and discontinuous definitions: 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{n_\alpha=\iiint_{V_\alpha}c^\dag_\alpha\,dV_\alpha=\iiint_{V_t}\varepsilon_\alpha \,c_\alpha\,dV_t \label{eq:def_n}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Advection-reaction-dispersion model}

\subsubsection{Governing differential equations}
 
The equations for tritium transport in a two-phase mixture of liquid and gas can be adapted from the existing literature on transport in a porous media and prior work on bubble column reactors\cite{malara_tritium_1995}, which the sparging system illustrated in \cref{fig:sparging_tank} resembles. Except when needed, the space ($z$) and time ($t$) dependences are implicit throughout this work for clarity. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Liquid: 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ARD_CT}
        \varepsilon_l \frac{\partial \CT}{\partial t} =  \underbrace{\varepsilon_l E_{l} \frac{\partial ^2 \CT}{\partial z^2}}_\text{liquid dispersion} + \underbrace{S_\mathrm{T_2}}_\text{tritium source} - \underbrace{a\,\JT}_{\text{mass transfer}}
    \end{equation}
    \item Gas: 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ARD_PT}
        \varepsilon_g \frac{1}{RT} \frac{\partial \PT}{\partial t} = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{RT}\frac{\partial (\,u_g\,\PT)}{\partial z}}_\text{gas advection} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_g E_{g} \frac{1}{RT}\frac{\partial ^2 \PT}{\partial z^2}}_\text{gas dispersion} + a\,\JT
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Where \CT and \PT are the concentrations in the liquid and gas phases, $\JT \:\mathrm{[mol/m^2/s]}$ is the mass exchange term coupling the two, $a \:\mathrm{[m^2/m^3]}$ is the specific bubble interface area per unit volume, $E_g$ and $E_l$ ($\mathrm{[m^2/s]}$) are dispersion coefficient for the gas and liquid phase respectively, $\varepsilon_g$ and $\varepsilon_l$ are the gas and liquid phase fractions, $u_g \: \mathrm{[m/s]}$ is the \textbf{superficial} gas phase velocity (not to be confused with the true bubbles velocity, previous work on the topic of tritium extraction can be ambiguous). This adaptation of \cite{gray_derivation_1975} intentionally omits molecular diffusion and mesoscale dispersion terms, as these are expected to be largely hidden by the system scale dispersion terms presented here.

The terms that appear in \cref{eq:ARD_CT,eq:ARD_PT} are elaborated in the following.

\subsubsection{Mass transfer}

      \label{fig:equilibrium}
    \end{subfigure}

% expose diffusivity, boundary layer model, then advective transport 
Transport through the liquid-gas interface created by bubbles is what directly allows tritium extraction from the liquid breeder. 
It relies primarily on diffusion, where the density gradient occurring at the interface drives a particle flux $\JT \:\mathrm{[mol/m^2/s]}$ from the tritium-rich liquid to the tritium-poor gas, hereafter the \textit{interfacial flux}. On top of pure diffusion, turbulence at the liquid-gas interface tends to promote mass exchange between phases. 
% It depends on two fundamental properties of the solute-solvant couple (here tritium-molten salt): diffusivity $D_T \:\mathrm{[m^2/s]}$ and solubility $K_s$  ($\mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa]}$ or $\mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa^{0.5}]}$ depending on the solubility law).
\\
Transport of solute gases through liquid-gas interfaces is commonly described by the Lewis-Whitman two-film model\cite{lewis_principles_1924} illustrated in \cref{fig:two_film_model}. The whole diffusive resistance to the transfer sits in the two films; the concentration is discontinuous at the interface itself, where the two sides stay at equilibrium. In general, the liquid film carries almost all of the resistance, so $\delta_g$ is neglected in this work and $c_\mathrm{T_2,gi}$ is taken as the bulk gas value\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}. The interfacial flux is therefore expressed using the liquid side mass transfer coefficient $h_l \:\mathrm{[m/s]}$ which characterized both molecular diffusion and turbulent transport through the liquid film. The interfacial flux is expressed as:


\begin{equation*}
    \JT = h_l \,(\CT-c_\mathrm{T_2,li})
\end{equation*}

Where $\CT \: \mathrm{[mol/m^3]}$ is the tritium concentration in the bulk liquid phase, $c_\mathrm{T_2,i}$ is the tritium concentration in the liquid at the liquid-gas interface.
\par
Concentration at the liquid-gas interface is assumed to be always at equilibrium\cite{ricapito_tritium_2011}, which in molten salts is given by the linear Henry's law as illustrated in \cref{fig:equilibrium}: $c_{T_2,li}=K_s \,P_{T_2}$, where $P_{T_2} \:\mathrm{[Pa]}$ is the tritium partial pressure in the gas phase, and $K_s \: \mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa]}$ is the Henry's solubility constant of the liquid. It follows:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:JT2}
    \JT = h_l \, (c_{T_2}-K_s\,\PT)
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:JT2}, \CT and \PT are the unknowns to be solved in the 1D-ARD model, coupled by \JT; $K_s$ is a salt property; $h_l$ is a parameter that depends on tritium diffusivity and fluid dynamic characteristics of the salt. 
% -> transition to next section

\subsubsection{Fluid transport}
% expose correlations
Advective transport through fluid motion is expected to influence, if not dominate, the tritium concentration and partial pressure profiles in the liquid and gas phases respectively. In a 2D or 3D model, it would require to solve for the velocity field of both phases. Because this work's purpose is to be exploratory, a good pragmatic choice is the simpler one-dimensional axial dispersion model presented by Levenspiel \cite{levenspiel_chemical_1999}, broadly used in chemical reactors modeling \cite{schluter_modeling_1992}. The gas upward velocity is described by the bubble's terminal velocity $u_g \:\mathrm{[m/s]}$, and the liquid and gas mixing effects are described by empirical dispersion coefficients $E_l$ and $E_g$ ($\mathrm{[m^2/s]}$). The temperature $T$ is assumed constant in time and space. \textbf{$E_l$ is the single liquid transport mechanism, it carries the recirculation physics} illustrated in \cref{fig:sparging_tank} and is therefore expected to be large; $E_g$ expresses as spread in the rise velocity of individual bubbles (e.g. due to a spread in bubble sizes), expected to be secondary. 
\par

The net bubble velocity gives rise to an advective term, while mixing is modelled by dispersion terms in 1D. Dispersion resembles molecular diffusion, as both phenomena have a Fickian behavior which tends to flatten the concentration profiles. They are however different from a physical point of view and shouldn't be mixed up: transport caused by phases mixing largely dominates molecular diffusion at macroscopic scales. This implies that \textbf{dispersion coefficients are orders of magnitude larger than diffusion coefficients}. 


\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\Cref{eq:ARD_CT,eq:ARD_PT} form a system of two partial differential equations coupled through \cref{eq:JT2}, called \textbf{1D Advection-Reaction-Dispersion (ARD)} model. It needs 4 boundary conditions (BCs) to have a unique solution.

\subsubsection{Derivation from mass conservation considerations}

This model is about gas sparging for tritium extraction from the liquid salt where it is bred, the life of a tritium atom that it aims to describe is as follows: (1) tritium gets born in the salt from neutron interaction (abstracted as a known source term $S_\mathrm{T_2}$); (2) transfers from the liquid to the gas phase (through an unknown interfacial flux \JT); (3) gets advected by the bubbles ($u_g$) up to the gas outlet where it is eventually considered as extracted. Note that tritium is constrained to leave the tank \textbf{solely via the gas phase}. It is a deliberate choice of this model not to consider other extraction mechanisms such as permeation from the liquid to the structural material or direct molecular diffusion from the liquid top free-surface to the sweep gas above. 

The tritium path (1),(2) and (3) translate into constraints on the evolution of the tritium inventory $\dot n_\alpha \ \mathrm{[mol/s]}$ in each phase, which will condition the choice of BCs. They are expressed as follows: 
\begin{align}
    \dotNTl &= \stackrel{(1)}{\iiint_V S_\mathrm{T_2} \,dV}\stackrel{(2)}{- \iiint_V a\JT \,dV} \label{eq:n_l_constraint}\\
    \dotNTg &= \stackrel{(3)}{A \,(u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{inlet}} - u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{outlet}})} + \stackrel{(2)}{\iiint_V a\JT \,dV} \label{eq:n_g_constraint}
\end{align}
Where $c_g \equiv\frac{\PT}{RT}$ is the concentration in the gas phase, $A$ is the base area of the tank and $H$ its height. Note $c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{inlet}}$ is kept to stay general, but is eventually null so that (1) is the only way tritium is introduced in the system (i.e. the sparging gas is pure He).


The BCs that enforce this constraint are obtained by integrating \cref{eq:ARD_CT,eq:ARD_PT} over the tank volume:
\begin{align}
    \dotNTl &= \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_V \varepsilon_g \,c_l \,dV =\iiint_V \varepsilon_g \, \frac{\partial c_l}{\partial t}\,dV  \nonumber\\
    &\stackrel{\eqref{eq:ARD_CT}}{=} A\,\bigg(\varepsilon_l\,E_l\,\frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=H} -\varepsilon_l\,E_l\,\frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0} \bigg)- \iiint_V a\JT \,dV  \label{eq:n_l_integrated}
\end{align}
Where $c_l\equiv \CT$ is the \ce{T2} concentration in the liquid phase, used in this derivation for consistency with the gas phase notation $c_g$. Similarly:
\begin{align}
    \dotNTg &\stackrel{\eqref{eq:ARD_PT}}{=}  A \,\bigg(u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0} - u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=H} \,+ \varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=H} -\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0} \bigg) + \iiint_V a\JT \,dV \label{eq:n_g_integrated}
\end{align}

In these expressions, it can be seen that mathematical dispersion terms can contribute to tritium leaving the tank, via a "dispersive current" $\varepsilon_\alpha\,E_\alpha\,\frac{\partial c_\alpha}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0,H}$, analogous to leakage due to molecular diffusion out of a system. Yet, in reality, physical mixing which dispersion terms describe doesn't make the phase being mixed "leak" at the boundaries of the tank. It is especially striking in the case of the liquid phase, which doesn't have a net flow and thus never enters nor leaves the system by any means (as illustrated by the blue dashed arrows in \cref{fig:sparging_tank}). 

Equating \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} and \cref{eq:n_g_integrated} yields the BCs that make compatible the mathematical formulation and the physics to be described: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item in the case of the liquid phase: it forces a zero concentration gradient at the boundaries: 
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=0,H}=0
        \end{equation*}
    
    \item in the case of the gas phase: a concentration gradient is expected to exist due to the non-zero advection term. The gas concentration at the inlet is known, \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} can thus be satisfied by allowing a discontinuity between the gas concentration there ($z=\mathrm{inlet}=0^-$) and the continuous concentration profile in the system. The resulting concentration jump introduces an advective transport contribution that cancels unphysical dispersion-induced leakage at the inlet. This type of 3rd type (Robin) BC is known as a \textit{Danckwerts boundary condition} in the chemical engineering literature: 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0^-}= u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0} -\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0}
        \end{equation*}
    
    The gas concentration at the outlet on the other hand is the unknown we typically want the model to predict, thus continuity is required here ($c_g\rvert_{z=H}=c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{outlet}}$), and \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} imposes a zero-gradient: 
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
        \end{equation*}
    
    This set of boundary conditions for the gas phase is typically refered to as "closed-closed" in the literature\cite{levenspiel_chemical_1999,mohan_experimental_2010}
\end{itemize} 

\subsubsection{Summary}
\Crefrange{eq:BC1}{eq:BC4} presents the set of 4 boundary conditions previously derived, that enforce the desired tritium extraction physics for the 1D ARD model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the gas inlet, the sparging gas is considered pure He free of any tritium ($c_g\rvert_{z=0^-}=0$), the Danckwert BC becomes:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC1}
        u_g\,\PT\rvert_{z=0} =\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial \PT}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0}
    \end{equation}
    \item At the top of the salt, unlike at the inlet, the gas flow is undisturbed, the tritium concentration stays flat at the transitions from the salt to the free head space:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC2}
        \frac{\partial\PT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
    \end{equation}
    \item The liquid phase is restrained to the tank volume, and tritium can only escape the system by being advected in the gas phase. In other words, the net tritium current arising from dispersion terms in \cref{eq:ARD_PT} must be null at the tank's boundaries. This is enforced by Neumann BCs:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC3}
        \frac{\partial\CT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=0}=0
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC4}
        \frac{\partial\CT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\Cref{eq:BC3} implies that no tritium is transported from the top of the liquid surface to the above head space gas. This assumption is discussed in section \ref{sec:sparging_assumptions}.

\subsection{Derived quantities}
Ultimately, what we are interested in is the evolution of the tritium inventory in the molten salt $n_{T_2,l} \:\mathrm{[molT_2]}$, which can be obtained from the profiles computed by the 1D ARD model. 

\subsubsection{Definition and properties} 

The profiles solved by the 1D ARD model are inherently space dependent, and because of the hydrostatic pressure in the tank $P_l(z)=P_\mathrm{top}+\rho_l\,g\,(H-z)$, all the hydrodynamic parameters are also space dependent. It will be useful in the rest of this work to represent an arbitrary spatially varying quantity $\psi(z)$ by its average over a volume $\alpha$, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:def_average}
    \boxed{\langle \psi \rangle^\alpha \equiv \frac{\iiint_{V_\alpha}\psi(\vec r) \,dV_\alpha}{V_\alpha}}
\end{equation}

When $\alpha$ is the liquid or gas phase ($l,g$) it should be specified in the notation, but we set the convention to omit it when it refers to the whole system (i.e. the tank $t=l \cup g$). Defining $H_\alpha\equiv\frac{V_\alpha}{A}$ and considering that $\iiint_{V_\alpha}\psi(\vec r) \,dV_\alpha=A\,\int_{H_\alpha}\psi(z)\,dz_\alpha$ since we are in 1D, it follows that:
\begin{align}
    \langle \varepsilon_l \rangle &= \frac{\int_{H_t}\varepsilon_l(z) \,dz_t}{H_t}\\
    \langle \CT \rangle^l &= \frac{\int_{H_l}\CT(z) \,dz_l}{H_l} 
\end{align}

From the generic definition of the phase fraction (\cref{eq:def_eps}) and the generic definition of the concentration (\cref{eq:def_c_generic}), it comes a few properties that justify why the volume average we just defined is handy to work with:
\begin{align}
    \langle \varepsilon_l \rangle& = \frac{V_l}{V_t} \\
    \langle \CT \rangle^l &= \frac{\NTl}{V_l} \\
    \langle \varepsilon_l\,\CT \rangle &\stackrel{\eqref{eq:def_n}}{=} \frac{\NTl}{V_t}=\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle \, \langle \CT \rangle^l \label{eq:prop_separable}
\end{align} 
This holds equivalently for the corresponding gas phase quantities ($\varepsilon_g,\PT$). Note that the average of \CT alone makes physical sense only when averaged over the volume of the liquid phase, for the reason mentioned in \cref{sec:generic_definitions}. The quantity $\langle \CT \rangle$ corresponds to a dilution of the liquid phase tritium, from a volume $V_l$ to a volume $V_t=V_l+V_g$.

\subsubsection{Quantity of interest}

\paragraph{Evolution of the tritium inventory}
The total tritium inventory present in the system at a certain time is the sum of the liquid and gas contributions:
\begin{equation}
    \NT \equiv \NTl+\NTg
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq:n_l_constraint,eq:n_g_constraint} being enforced by the boundary conditions, it follows that during an \textit{extraction phase}, when sparging is turned ON and the neutron source that breeds tritium is OFF (\ST=0), the tritium inventory in the system evolves as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dotNT}
    \dotNT = -A\,u_g\,\frac{\PT}{RT} \bigg\rvert_\mathrm{outlet}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Quantifying the extraction kinetics}
\label{sec:extraction_kinetics}
For the same sparging system, \dotNT varies with time and the tritium concentration, in that regard it is not a good metric to quantify the kinetics of the extraction phase. Instead, we can try to guess what law the extraction phase should follow, by considering the volume average of the ARD equation (\cref{eq:ARD_CT}):
\begin{equation}
    \langle \varepsilon_l \frac{\partial\CT}{\partial t} \rangle=-\langle a\,\JT \rangle 
\end{equation}

Note the dispersion term vanishes upon integration, as per our BCs (\cref{eq:BC3,eq:BC4}). Now applying a property of space averaging (\cref{eq:prop_separable}), considering that $\JT \approx h_l\,\CT$ and assuming that $a(z)\,h_l(z)$ is sufficiently uniform to write $\langle a\,h_l\,\CT\rangle\approx\langle a\,h_l\rangle \langle\CT\rangle^l$, we get that $\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\CT\rangle^l \approx -\langle a\,h_l\rangle \langle\CT\rangle^l$. The solution of this differential equation is of the form: 
\begin{equation}
    \langle\CT\rangle^l(t)\approx \langle\CT\rangle^l(0)\cdot e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}
\end{equation}

This is a hint that the average concentration and therefore the tritium inventory are expected to follow an exponential decay. Because the ARD equations are linear, the magnitude of the tritium concentration doesn't impact the extraction kinetics, $\tau$ is therefore initial condition agnostic. 

\textbf{In the rest of this work, $\tau$ is used as a metric to characterizes the kinetic of the extraction phase}. Here, we made the assumptions that the term $K_s\,\PT$ of the interfacial flux (\cref{eq:JT2}) is negligible, it is referred to as the \textit{small partial pressure} (SPP) approximation. When this assumption is valid (conditions are investigated in \cref{sec:impact_of_sat_Pi}), we have a known expression for $\tau$, given by:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\tau_\mathrm{SPP}\equiv\frac{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle}{\langle a\,h_l\rangle}}
    \label{eq:tau_SPP}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closure relations and correlations}

\input{tab_correlations}

The parameters that appear as coefficients in the ARD equations (\cref{eq:ARD_CT,eq:ARD_PT}) need to be obtained from closure relations, based on more fundamental parameters that are actually controlled or measured in an experiment, like temperature, pressure, gas flow rate or tank geometry. 
All the closure relations used in this work are reported in \cref{tab:correlations} and \cref{tab:correlations_hydro}. Specifically, this set of closure relations will be used in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging} to simulate gas sparging in the LIBRA Pi experiment. 

In the following, we focus justify the hydrodynamic closures reported in \cref{tab:correlations_hydro}, by elaborating on the way individual bubble parameters are closed using sparging gas throughput, 2 correlations on the bubble's initial diameter and terminal velocity, and the ideal gas law.

\paragraph{Superficial velocity}

The sparger injects a fixed molar flow rate $\dot n_g$ of helium. At a height $z$, this gas occupies a volume dictated by the local pressure inside a bubble, $P_g(z)$, and by the temperature, assumed uniform (\cref{as:uniform_temp}). The ideal gas law gives the local volumetric flow rate:
\begin{equation}
    \dot V_g(z) = \frac{\dot n_g \, R \, T}{P_g(z)}
\end{equation}

The \textit{superficial} velocity is this volumetric flow spread over the whole base area $A$ of the tank, i.e. the velocity the gas would have if it alone occupied the column:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:app_u_g}
    u_g(z) \equiv \frac{\dot V_g(z)}{A} = \frac{\dot n_g\,R\,T}{A\,P_g(z)}
\end{equation}

It is not the velocity of an individual bubble $v_g$. The two are related by the gas fraction. The pressure inside a bubble is the local hydrostatic pressure of the salt plus the Laplace overpressure of its curved interface:
\begin{align}
    P_l(z) &= P_\mathrm{top} + \rho_l\,g\,(H-z) \label{eq:app_P_l}\\
    P_g(z) &= P_l(z) + \frac{4\,\sigma_l}{d_b(z)} \label{eq:app_P_g}
\end{align}

It should be noted that the Laplace term is small in the conditions of this work: at the sparger of the LIBRA Pi case it amounts to $101\ \mathrm{Pa}$ against $1.2\cdot10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ of hydrostatic pressure, i.e. $0.08\ \%$. We can therefore choose to neglect it and approximate $P_g\approx P_l$.

The bubble size $d_b$ can be estimated from hydrostatic bubble expansion, where we approximate $P_g\approx P_l$ and apply the ideal gas law for a bubble with constant gas inventory (no coalescence,\cref{as:no_coalescence}) while neglecting the gas temperature drop as the bubble expands (isothermal expansion,\cref{as:uniform_temp}):
\begin{equation}
    d_b(z)=d_{b0}\left(\frac{P_l(0)}{P_l(z)}\right)^{1/3}
\end{equation}

$d_{b0}$ is obtained from a correlation on the gas volumetric flow rate at the sparger nozzles and the nozzle diameter itself, derived by \textcite{kanai_establishment_2017} and reported in \textcite{evans_physics_2026} as indicated in \cref{tab:correlations_hydro} (the original paper couldn't be accessed).

\Cref{eq:app_u_g} is the origin of the $z$ dependence exploited in \cref{sec:impact_of_pressure}: as a bubble rises, $P_l$ falls, the gas expands and $u_g$ grows. The bubble diameter follows the same decompression at constant bubble mass, $d_b \propto P_l^{-1/3}$.



\paragraph{Gas fraction}

We use the standard relation between superficial and interstitial velocity: $u_g = \varepsilon_g\,v_b$. The interstitial velocity or "intrinsic gas velocity" $v_b$ is obtained from a correlation on bubble terminal velocity for He in FliNaK molten salt by \textcite{chavez_experimental_2021}.

A simple reasoning can be used to get more intuition about this relation. The gas fraction $\varepsilon_g$ is the volume of gas per unit volume of tank (\cref{eq:def_eps}). Consider a slice of column of thickness $\mathrm{d}z$ and base area $A$. Bubbles cross it at their terminal velocity $v_b$, assumed constant (\cref{as:vb_is_constant}), so each bubble spends a time $\mathrm{d}z/v_b$ inside the slice. In steady state, the volume of gas held in the slice is the volumetric flow times that residence time:
\begin{equation}
    \delta V_g = \dot V_g(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{v_b}
\end{equation}
Dividing by the slice volume $A\,\mathrm{d}z$ gives the gas fraction, and recognising \cref{eq:app_u_g}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:app_eps_g}
    \boxed{\varepsilon_g(z) = \frac{\dot V_g(z)}{A\,v_b} = \frac{u_g(z)}{v_b}
    = \frac{\dot n_g\,R\,T}{A\,v_b\,P_g(z)}}
\end{equation}

This lands on the standard bubble column holdup relation $u_g = \varepsilon_g\,v_b$, and it makes explicit the distinction the model relies on: the superficial velocity is smaller than the intrinsic bubble velocity by exactly the gas fraction, which in this work is of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$. \textbf{Paying special attention to which of them a correlation is built on is of prior importance}, since confusing them misplaces a quantity by three to four orders of magnitude. For example, correlations on gas and liquid dispersion in this work are built on the superficial velocity $u_g$ as indicated in \cref{tab:correlations_hydro}.

Finally, the specific interfacial area follows from the gas fraction and the bubble diameter, assuming spherical bubbles of surface $\pi d_b^2$ and volume $\frac{\pi}{6}d_b^3$:
\begin{equation}
    a(z) = \varepsilon_g(z)\,\frac{\pi\,d_b^2}{\frac{\pi}{6}d_b^3}
         = \frac{6\,\varepsilon_g(z)}{d_b(z)}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Assumptions of the model}
The main assumptions used throughout this work are listed in the following, starting with the most impactful ones:
\label{sec:sparging_assumptions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \assumption{Tritium is only present as molecular \ce{T2}}: this is equivalent to assuming that the redox of the salt is perfectly controlled, preventing newborn tritium to form tritium fluoride (\ce{TF}). Assuming \ce{T2} as the dominating species is compatible with the recent results of the BABY 1L tritium breeding experiment in \ce{ClLiF}, where more than 96\% of the tritium was collected as \ce{T2} and \ce{HT}\cite{delaporte-mathurin_baby_2026}. The assumed chemical speciation of tritium greatly determines its mass transfer properties: results based on ab initio molecular dynamics  suggest that the molecular diffusion coefficient of \ce{T2} is 3-5 times higher than that of \ce{TF} and it is less soluble\cite{lam_impact_2021}. Note this assumption is not inherently tied to the model, other chemical forms of tritium could be considered simply by changing the transport coefficients used by the model.
    \item \assumption{the temperature field is uniform\label{as:uniform_temp}}: often we don't have spatial resolution for the salt temperature, it is the case in the LIBRA Pi experiment. It is the only model input that can't be controlled nor measured precisely in the experiment. The approach then is to repeat the simulation for a large distribution of possible temperatures, which should give lower and upper bounds on the predicted extraction performance. As a consequence, bubbles and liquid are considered always at thermal equilibrium, which implies that the liquid brings some heat to the bubbles as they expand toward the top of the tank, i.e. the bubbles expansion is non-adiabatic. We can then write for a bubble: $P_bV_b=n_bRT=\mathrm{constant}$ thus $V_b(z)=V_{b0} \frac{P_{b0}}{P_b(z)}$
    \item \assumption{constant bubble rising velocity $v_b$ \label{as:vb_is_constant}}: the terminal velocity is a result of a quasi-static equilibrium between buoyancy forces and drag forces. In wobbling/ellipsoid regimes this equilibrium barely changes with the bubble size, it stays within $0.25\pm0.05 \ \mathrm{m/s}$\cite{chavez_experimental_2021}. Bubble size varies as $d_b\propto P_l^{-1/3}$, so growth due to decompression is already not large and the shift it induces in the forces equilibrium can be neglected;
    \item \assumption{no bubbles coalescence\label{as:no_coalescence}}: at such low superficial gas velocity, void fractions falls below 1\%, we can reasonably expect the bubbles not to interact with other bubbles\cite{taylor_implementation_2022}. Under nominal operating conditions in LIBRA Pi the gas fraction is $\sim5\cdot10^{-4}$ and the bubble diameter 6.0 mm, which places the bubbles about $\frac{\sfrac{6 \ \mathrm{mm}}{2}}{(5\cdot10^{-4})^{\sfrac{1}{3}}}\sqrt{\Pi} = 67$ mm apart, i.e. \textbf{ten bubble diameters}
    \item \assumption{no permeation in structural material nor diffusion at free surface\label{as:no_permeation}}: this is a modeling choice to only retain interaction with gas bubbles as a tritium extraction mechanism, this gives a conservative estimate of the tritium extraction speed. Permeation can be estimated by coupling with a model for tritium transport is solid media, this is done in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_permeation}; 
    \item \assumption{the contribution of \ce{T2} to bubble pressure is negligible\label{as:T2_press_is_negligible}}: the He pressure in a bubble is of the order of $10^5 \ \mathrm{Pa}$, the amount of tritium that can be present in the system using the neutron source of the LIBRA Pi experiment is of the order of $0.1\ \mathrm{T/neutron}\cdot10^9\ \mathrm{neutron/s}\cdot10 \ \mathrm{hours}\approx 6\cdot10^{-11}\ \mathrm{molT}$, which if it were to be transferred all at once on the gas phase would results in a \ce{T2} pressure of $\frac{(3\cdot10^{-11}\ \mathrm{molT_2})\,R\,(873\ \mathrm{K})}{10^{-4}\cdot (0.1 \ \mathrm{m^3})}=0.2 \ \mathrm{Pa}\ll 10^5 \ \mathrm{Pa}$;
\end{itemize}

\section{Analytical solution of the ARD equations}
\label{sec:analytical_solution}
To be able to understand the numerical results that will be obtained later on, it is insightful to first derive an analytical solution, at the expense of some simplifications. The assumptions made here will be discussed and characterized in \cref{sec:sparging_expl}. 

\subsection{Premise: case of a single static bubble}
Let us consider the virtual case of a single He bubble $b$, immersed in a static liquid of tritium concentration \CT. The evolution of the tritium inventory $n_{\mathrm{T_2},b}$ in the bubble is governed by:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nb}
    \frac{dn_{\mathrm{T_2},b}}{dt} = A_b\,\JT
\end{equation}

Writing it in terms of tritium partial pressure using the ideal gas law and expanding the right term, it becomes:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pb_ODE}
    \frac{V_b}{RT}\frac{d\PT}{dt} = A_b\,h_l\,(\CT-K_s\PT)
\end{equation}

We are assuming a spherical bubble of diameter $d_b$, the specific interfacial area of the bubble is $a_b=\frac{A_b}{V_b}=\frac{6}{d_b}$ (not to be confused with the specific interfacial area of the gas phase as a whole $a=\varepsilon_ga_b$). We make the assumption that \assumption{\CT barely changes for the duration the bubble spends in the system \label{as:c_is_constant}} (\cref{as:c_is_constant}), such that it can be considered constant. 
Solving this ODE yields:
\begin{equation}
    \PT(t)=\frac{\CT}{K_s}\,(1-e^{-a_b\,h_l\,R\,T\,K_s\,t})
\end{equation}

Let us define the bubble saturation time constant:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_\mathrm{sat}\equiv \frac{1}{a_b\,h_l\,R\,T\,K_s}
\end{equation}
Eventually, the bubble is removed of the liquid after a residence time $t_{res}$, it leaves the system with a tritium concentration $\PT(t_{res})=\frac{\CT}{K_s}\,(1-e^{-\frac{t_{res}}{\tau_\mathrm{sat}}})$. We can write the quantity in the exponential term as a dimensionless number $\Pi$ defined as the \textit{ratio of the bubble residence time to its saturation time constant}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Pi_def_micro}
    \boxed{\Pi\equiv \frac{t_{res}}{\tau_\mathrm{sat}}= a_b\,h_l\,R\,T\,K_s\,t_{res}}
\end{equation}

It can be interpreted as a measure of "how close the bubble is to reaching equilibrium with the surrounding liquid", the equilibrium being $\PT=\frac{c_l}{K_s}$. 

\subsection{Solution for a sparging system}
Now let us adapt the single bubble case to a whole swarm of bubbles rising in the liquid and eventually escaping it (i.e. a sparging system). The reasoning is as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The speed $v_b$ of the bubble, assumed constant (\cref{as:vb_is_constant}), allows the change of variable $t=\frac{z}{v_b}$ and yields a bubble residence time $t_{res}=\frac{H}{v_b}$. 
    \item Bubble's properties now vary with pressure as bubbles rise:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $V_b$ becomes $V_b=V_g=\varepsilon_g(z)\,V_t$ which is not constant, mass conservation applied to the bubble must account for that. Changing variable, substituting $a=\frac{A_b}{V_t}$ and $u_g=\varepsilon_g\,v_b$, \cref{eq:Pb_ODE} is re-written:
        \begin{align}
            \label{eq:Pb_ODE_sparger}
            &\frac{1}{RT}\frac{d(V_b\,\PT)}{dt} = A_b\,h_l\,(\CT-K_s\PT) \nonumber\\
            \Leftrightarrow &\frac{1}{RT}\frac{d(u_g\,\PT)}{dz} = a\,h_l\,(\CT-K_s\PT) 
        \end{align}
        \item $\Pi$ becomes a function of position. The definition given in \cref{eq:Pi_def_micro} involves microscopic scale (bubble specific) quantities considered constant. In the case of a sparging system, using same substitutions as above yields an expression in terms of macroscopic scale quantities:
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:Pi_def_macro}
        \boxed{\Pi(z) = \frac{R\,T\,K_s\,a(z)\,h_l(z)}{u_g(z)}\,H}
        \end{equation}
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \Cref{as:c_is_constant} has two implications:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \CT(z) seen by the bubble as it rises is well approximated by $\langle \CT\rangle^l$, i.e. \assumption{the concentration in the liquid phase can be considered uniform \label{as:c_is_uniform}} (\cref{as:c_is_uniform})
        \item $\langle \CT\rangle^l$ itself can be considered constant during the bubble's life, i.e. \assumption{the residence time of the bubble in the system is much shorter than the extraction timescale \label{as:short_res_time}} (\cref{as:short_res_time}): $\frac{H}{v_b}\ll\tau$
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Note on $\Pi$}

The physical intuition is: $\Pi(z)$ gives the outlet saturation a bubble would have if the \textbf{whole column} were in the hydrodynamic conditions found at height $z$. It is distinct from what a bubble actually experiences on its way from the sparger up to $z$ which is the \textbf{accumulation} of these local contributions, written:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pi_cumul}
    \Pi^\star(z)\equiv\frac{1}{H}\int_0^z\Pi(s)\,ds=R\,T\,K_s\int_0^z\frac{a(s)\,h_l(s)}{u_g(s)}\,ds
\end{equation}
so that $\Pi^\star(H)= R\,T\,K_s\,\int_0^H\frac{a(z)\,h_l(z)}{u_g(z)}dz=\langle\Pi\rangle$, and $\Pi^\star(z)=\langle\Pi\rangle\,\frac{z}{H}$ when the hydrodynamic parameters are uniform along the column.

As will be elaborated throughout this work, \PI is a pivotal quantity to characterize a gas sparging system. Its definition in terms of microscopic quantities (\cref{eq:Pi_def_micro}) is the reference to understand what it really is and the parameters that drive it. 

\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \langle\Pi\rangle=R\,T\,K_s\,\int_0^H\frac{a(z)\,h_l(z)}{u_g(z)}dz
    \end{equation}
    The saturation number $\langle\Pi\rangle$ describes the \textbf{bubble saturation} as it leaves the tank, resulting from all the conditions $\Pi(z)$ encountered along its way
\end{keyresult} 


\paragraph{Derivation}
Using the uniform \CT assumption (\cref{as:c_is_uniform}), \cref{eq:Pb_ODE_sparger} reduces to:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PT_steady}
    \frac{1}{R\,T}\frac{d\,(u_g\,\PT)}{dz}=a\,h_l\,\left(\langle\CT\rangle^l-K_s\,\PT\right)
\end{equation}
Note this lands to the gas ARD equation (\cref{eq:ARD_PT}) in steady state and without the dispersion term. The fact that the gas phase is in (quasi-)steady state here is a consequence of the assumption of a short bubble residence time (\cref{as:short_res_time}). We also don't account for a possible spread in the bubble's velocity, assuming that \assumption{gas dispersion is negligible in front of gas advection\label{as:no_gas_dispersion}}(\cref{as:no_gas_dispersion}). This is justified by the gas Peclet number, which compares advective to dispersive transport along the column: $\mathrm{Pe}_g=v_b\,H/E_g=5.2\cdot10^{4}$ at the nominal LIBRA Pi operating point, so the dispersive term is $1/\mathrm{Pe}_g\simeq2\cdot10^{-5}$ of the advective one. 

We further assume that \assumption{the hydrodynamic parameters are well approximated by their spatial average\label{as:uniform_hydrodynamic}} (\cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic}), so that $u_g$, $a$ and $h_l$ come out of the derivative and the cumulated saturation grows linearly, $\Pi^\star(z)=\langle\Pi\rangle\,\frac{z}{H}$ (\cref{eq:Pi_cumul}). Gas dispersion being neglected, \cref{eq:BC1} imposes a tritium free sparging gas at the inlet, $\PT(0)=0$, and \cref{eq:PT_steady} integrates into an analytical expression for the concentration profile in the gas phase:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PT_profile}
    \PT(z)=\frac{\langle \CT\rangle^l}{K_s}\,\left(1-e^{-\Pi^\star(z)}\right)
\end{equation}
This is the single bubble result of \cref{eq:Pi_def_micro} transposed along the column: the bubble relaxes towards the equilibrium partial pressure $\frac{\langle \CT\rangle^l}{K_s}$, with the cumulated saturation $\Pi^\star(z)$ playing the role that $\frac{t}{\tau_\mathrm{sat}}$ played for a single bubble.

The now known gas concentration profile can be evaluated at the gas outlet and plugged into the expression of the outgoing tritium flux (\cref{eq:dotNT}) to work out the evolution of the tritium inventory:
\begin{equation}
    \dotNT=-A\,u_g\frac{\PT}{RT}\bigg\rvert_\mathrm{outlet}=-\frac{A\,u_g\,\langle \CT\rangle^l}{R\,T\,K_s}\,\left(1-e^{-\langle\Pi\rangle}\right)
\end{equation}
Given the assumed negligible bubble residence time (\cref{as:short_res_time}), we can consider that every tritium that goes into the gas phase is immediately evacuated out of the system, therefore $\NTg\ll\NTl \Rightarrow \NTl\approx\NT$. Simplifying $\langle \CT\rangle^l=\frac{\NTl}{V_l}=\frac{\NTl}{\langle \varepsilon_l\rangle V_t}$ with $V_t=A\,H$, and substituting $u_g=\frac{R\,T\,K_s\,\langle a\,h_l\rangle\,H}{\langle\Pi\rangle}$ from the definition of $\Pi$ (\cref{eq:Pi_def_macro}) yields:
\begin{equation}
    \dotNT=-\frac{\langle a\,h_l \rangle}{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle}\,\frac{1-e^{-\langle\Pi\rangle}}{\langle\Pi\rangle}\,\NT
\end{equation}

Solving yields an exponential decay, as obtained previously under the small partial pressure approximation (\cref{sec:extraction_kinetics}), here generalized for any partial pressure regime. Recognizing the SPP extraction time $\tau_\mathrm{SPP}=\frac{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle}{\langle a\,h_l\rangle}$ (\cref{eq:tau_SPP}), the extraction time reads:
\begin{keyresult}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tau_ana}
    \tau =\tau_\mathrm{SPP}\,\frac{\langle\Pi\rangle}{1-e^{-\langle\Pi\rangle}}
\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

It is possible to get rid of \Cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic} by carrying a more complete derivation, as done in \cref{app:tau_full}: $a$, $h_l$ and $u_g$ are allowed to vary along the column, this gives an exact expression of $\tau$ in integral form (\cref{eq:tau_full}), which collapses back onto \cref{eq:tau_ana} once the profiles flatten. This more general expression is however more complex and can't be expressed in a simple algebraic form. The simpler \cref{eq:tau_ana} is therefore retained as the \textbf{reference analytical solution in the rest of this work}, denoted \Tana for distinction with values that might be obtained numerically.

\subsection{Overview}
The 1D-ARD equations require as input some tritium transport and fluid dynamics parameters that are non-trivially linked to known operating and design parameters. As illustrated in \cref{fig:model_overview}, the implementation of the model is two staged: known parameters are fed into (1) a closure relations resolution stage, which computes the parameters needed by (2) the 1D-ARD equations, which compute the space and time dependence of the tritium concentrations ($\PT(z,t),\CT(z,t$). Quantities of interest can then be derived from these.

\subsection{Closure relations resolution}
There are several available correlations in the literature for the same physical quantity, and the choice of which correlation to use is sometimes non-trivial: it changes depending on what parameters are already known and in what range they are. 

For example, one might choose to compute the gas fraction $\varepsilon_g$ from a correlation based on the gas flow rate and some properties of the flow; an other approach is to first fix the bubble terminal velocity $v_g$ with a correlation and afterwards obtain the gas fraction from the gas flow rate by simply using a fundamental property of the superficial velocity ($u_g=\frac{\dot V_g}{A}=\varepsilon_g \,v_g$). Here it can be seen that the triplet ($\dot V_g$, $\varepsilon_g$, $v_g$) presents a single degree of freedom to be fixed by a correlation, $\dot V_g$ being an input: which quantity $\varepsilon_g$ or $v_g$ is the correlation-dependent one is up to the user's choice. In general this can lead to a variety of correlations combinations, each equally valid. 


In particular, the topic of gas sparging in molten salts presents large unknowns (e.g. tritium transport properties, see \cref{fig:transport_params}), and it is the purpose of this model to explore them. It was therefore designed to offer flexibility as to the closure relations used and the assumptions they are built upon. 

This flexibility is achieved by dynamically resolving the dependencies between all the parameters of the model every time a new simulation is launched, based on a set of closure relations and known parameters specified by the user for this specific simulation. Here, a \textit{parameters} can be a physical quantity (e.g. temperature) or more broadly any quantity used to describes the system (e.g. gas fraction, dimensionless numbers), it doesn't have to be a scalar, some parameters have a spatially varying profile (the pressure dependent ones); a \textit{closure relations} is any relation between parameters (e.g. correlation, state equation, definition).

Each closure relation that gives a parameter $p_j$ is a function $f_j$ of $n$ other parameters $p_i$ ($i=1,...,n\neq j$). The $p_i$ are either known (given by the user or already computed), or they have to be obtained as the output of an other closure relation $f_i$. The problem of dynamically resolving this potentially complex network of interdependent parameters can be described using the formalism of mathematical graphs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item each parameter $p_j$ is a \textit{node} $j$ of the graph;
    \item the closure relation $f_j$ that computes $p_j$ from other parameters $p_i$ gives the \textit{ingoing links}, from each node $i=1,...,n$ to node $j$;
    \item the closure relations $f_k(...,p_j,...)$ that require $p_j$ as an input give the \textit{outgoing links}, from node $j$ to each node $k$.
\end{itemize}   
An example of the typical dependencies of the sparging model is given in \cref{fig:closure_graph}, obtained using the closure relations presented in \cref{sec:sparging_maths}. Note that a node $j$ can be computed (i.e. is \textit{discovered}) only if the $n$ nodes it depends on have been discovered already. This prohibits cyclic dependencies, where a node needs itself to be computed, i.e. where $p_j=f_j(p_i,...)=f_j(f_i(p_j,...),...)$.

Eventually, what is required by the sparging model are the ARD parameters that appear in the 1D ARD equations. The intermediate parameters that were used to derive the latter are valuable to understand the model. The input parameters are considered to be independently controlled by the user in the actual sparging experiment, they are the "bedrock" level of the graph in the sense that they cannot be obtained from closure relations. Computing the ARD parameters boils down to discovering the above described oriented graph, it is achieved by the closure relations resolution stage by following this sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the user specifies a set of known parameters (nodes) $N=\{p_i\}$ and closure relations (links) $L=\{f_k\}$ The known parameters can be input, but also intermediate or ARD parameters if the user wants to short-circuit some closure relations;
    \item the graph search algorithm is given a list of nodes it has to discover $\hat N=\{p_k, k=\mathrm{ARD \ parameters}\}$;
    \item for each element of $\hat N$, it recursively goes upstream the ingoing links leading to the required node $k$, following the set of links given in $L$, until reaching a known node $i$, which it uses to compute all the nodes on the way back from $i$ to $k$;
    \item each node thereby computed is added to the list of discovered nodes $N$ until it includes all the required nodes, when $\hat N \subset N$
\end{enumerate}
The parameters resolution algorithm will fail if the user provides a set of known parameters and relations that is insufficient to compute the ARD parameters (\textit{incomplete}). 

The \verb|from_parameters()| method of the \verb|SimulationInput| class implements this graph discovery, the recursive part of the algorithm is summarized in \cref{fig:graph_search}. An example resolution of the closure relations used in the LIBRA Pi sparging model in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging} can be found in \cref{fig:closure_graph}.

\subsection{Numerical scheme for the ARD equations}
\label{sec:numerical_scheme}

The ARD equations (\cref{eq:ARD_CT,eq:ARD_PT}) are discretised and solved with the finite element method. The weak formulation is obtained by multiplying each equation by a test function $v_c$ and $v_y$ and integrating over the column, the two dispersion terms being integrated by parts so that the zero-gradient conditions of \cref{eq:BC2,eq:BC3,eq:BC4} are enforced naturally, by dropping the resulting boundary terms.

The gas phase unknown is not the partial pressure itself but the \ce{T2} molar fraction $y_\mathrm{T_2}$, with $\PT=P_g(z)\,y_\mathrm{T_2}$, as it is the practice in bubble column reactor modeling\cite{malara_tritium_1995}. Writing $\Delta t$ for the time step and $(c^{k+1},y^{k+1})$ for the fields at $t_{k+1}$, the discrete system solved at each step is:

\begin{align}
\label{eq:weak_form}
    F = &\stackrel{\mathrm{(1a)}}{\int_0^H \varepsilon_l\,\frac{c^{k+1}-c^{k}}{\Delta t}\,v_c \,\mathrm{d}z} + \stackrel{\mathrm{(1b)}}{\int_0^H \frac{\varepsilon_g\,P_g}{R\,T}\,\frac{y^{k+1}-y^{k}}{\Delta t}\,v_y \,\mathrm{d}z} \nonumber\\
    &+ \stackrel{\mathrm{(2a)}}{\int_0^H \varepsilon_l\,E_l\,\nabla c^{k+1}\cdot\nabla v_c \,\mathrm{d}z} + \stackrel{\mathrm{(2b)}}{\int_0^H \frac{\varepsilon_g\,E_g}{R\,T}\,\nabla\!\left(P_g\,y^{k+1}\right)\cdot\nabla v_y \,\mathrm{d}z} \nonumber\\
    &+ \stackrel{\mathrm{(3)}}{\int_0^H \frac{1}{R\,T}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(u_g\,P_g\,y^{k+1}\right) v_y \,\mathrm{d}z} + \stackrel{\mathrm{(4)}}{\int_0^H a\,\JT\,(v_c - v_y) \,\mathrm{d}z} - \stackrel{\mathrm{(5)}}{\int_0^H S_\mathrm{T_2}\,v_c \,\mathrm{d}z} \nonumber\\
    &+ \stackrel{\mathrm{(6)}}{\left[\frac{u_g}{R\,T}\,\left(P_g\,y^{k+1} - \PT\rvert_\mathrm{inlet}\right) v_y\right]_{z=0}} \;=\; 0
\end{align}

With (1a) and (1b) the transient terms for each phase; (2a) and (2b) the dispersion terms; (3) the advection term of the gas phase, written in conservative form to accounts for gas expansion; (4) the interfacial coupling term; (5) the neutron irradiation source term; and (6) the Danckwerts condition of \cref{eq:BC1}, imposed weakly as a boundary term. With a tritium-free sparging gas, $\PT\rvert_\mathrm{inlet}=0$. Note that the two equations share the same interfacial term with opposite signs, through the factor $(v_c-v_y)$, so that the transfer between phases is conserved: whatever leaves the liquid enters the gas.

Space is discretised on a uniform mesh of $N_x=H/\Delta x$ cells, over which both unknowns are represented by Lagrange elements of degree 1. Time is solved with the backward Euler scheme, first order and unconditionally stable, whose systematic $+\Delta t/2$ bias on the fitted extraction time is derived and removed in \cref{sec:numerical_error}. 

The resulting system is assembled by DOLFINx and solved with a Newton method at each time step. The ARD equations and the boundary conditions are linear, therefore \cref{eq:weak_form} is linear in $(c_\mathrm{T_2},y_\mathrm{T_2})$ making the Newton iterations converge in a single step. That linearity is also why the extraction time is independent of the initial inventory, and why the Monte Carlo studies of \cref{sec:sparging_expl,sec:LIBRAPi_sparging} are affordable. No stabilization technique is used, the mesh Peclet criterion of \cref{sec:numerical_error} is what fixes $\Delta x$.

A spatial and temporal convergence study of the LIBRA Pi case (\cref{tab:model_input}) was performed, it is shown in \cref{fig:convergence}. The corresponding results are reported in \cref{tab:convergence_gci}. The observed order of convergence is $0.998$ in time and $1.999$ in space, as expected of a backward Euler scheme and of P1 elements, and the measured ratio $e_\Delta/(\Delta t/\tau)$ is $0.501$. On the finest discretisations the Roache grid convergence index is $0.157\ \%$ in time and $1.2\times10^{-6}\ \%$ in space, suggesting that \textbf{the total discretisation error on $\tau$ is entirely set by the time step through \cref{eq:e_delta}, and the mesh contributes nothing}.

The fact that the mesh contributes nothing can be explained by the intrinsic spatial dynamics of the ARD equations. The liquid phase has no advective term (\cref{eq:ARD_CT}), so no Peclet number can be defined for it. The liquid $E_l$ never sets the requirement, for the simple reason that dispersion only smooths, so the concentration profile can never be sharper than the interfacial source term $a\,h_l\,(\langle\CT\rangle^l-K_s\PT)$ that forces it. That term carries the gas phase length scales in \PT and $a\,h_l$: the length scale of the prior is dictated by the ratio $\frac{u_g}{E_g}$; the latter varies smoothly with hydrostatic pressure and is therefore described well enough by a coarse mesh. The limiting case $E_l\rightarrow0$, where each height decouples and the liquid simply inherits the gas profile, is therefore the worst case, and it is still governed by the gas. Therefore, \textbf{the spatial dynamics of the ARD equations are dictated by the gas phase characteristics only}, through the ratio $\frac{u_g}{E_g}$, suggesting that the mesh size should be index on the gas phase Peclet number $\mathrm{Pe}\equiv\frac{u_g\Delta x}{E_g}$. In the LIBRA Pi case, bubbles don't saturate much (relatively low \PI), the \PT profile is therefore smooth enough to be well described by even a coarse mesh that has a $\mathrm{Pe=2}$, explaining why the \textbf{temporal discretization error dominates the total discretization error}. Still, it should be noted that more extreme cases (e.g. high bubble saturation and poor mixing ) might lead to sharper \PT profile, requiring a lower Pe.


The conclusions of this convergence study are as follow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The temporal resolution decides the discretization error}: the time step can be indexed on the extraction time, which dictates the temporal dynamics of the system. It can be evaluated prior to a simulation using \Tana. In the rest of this work, a good compromise between accuracy and computational efficiency was chosen at $\Delta t = 10^{-2}\,\tau$, which gives $e_\Delta\approx+0.5\,\%$;
    \item \textbf{A coarse mesh size is acceptable as long as $\tau$ is concerned}: it is an integral of the outlet flux, which in the partial pressure limited regime does not depend on the shape of the \PT profile at all. \textbf{$\mathrm{Pe}=2$ is therefore sufficient, it is adopted in the rest of this work}. Only cases where the quantity of interest is $\PT(z)$ itself at large $\langle\Pi\rangle$ might require a finer mesh
\end{itemize}

It can be shown that the temporal discretization error is \textbf{systematic rather than statistical}: the backward Euler scheme reproduces the inventory decay as an exact exponential, only with a time constant $\tau_\Delta\simeq\tau+\frac{\Delta t}{2}$, so it always over-estimates the extraction time by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:e_delta}
    e_\Delta \equiv \frac{\tau_\Delta-\tau}{\tau}\simeq\frac{\Delta t}{2\,\tau}
\end{equation}
which is first order in $\Delta t$ and independent of the operating point, since it only involves the ratio of the time step to the extraction time being measured. A short proof is given in \cref{app:dt_bias}. Being systematic, this bias can be subtracted rather than merely bounded, and every comparison of a fitted extraction time against the analytical solution in the following chapters is made on $\tau_\Delta-\frac{\Delta t}{2}$, so that \textbf{what is left is the model error alone}.

The analytical solution derived in \cref{sec:analytical_solution} rests on a set of assumptions that the numerical 1D ARD model does not make. A \textbf{virtual} reference case is therefore built in which all of them are satisfied, so that any remaining discrepancy can be attributed to the implementation rather than to the modelling: we use the ARD input corresponding to the LIBRA Pi experiment (see \cref{tab:model_input}), except for the \textbf{tank height which is divided by ten} to satisfy \cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic}, and for the solubility constant $K_s$ which takes two values in order to probe the implementation correctness in both partial pressure regimes. \begin{enumerate}
    \item The liquid concentration is uniform (\cref{as:c_is_uniform}): the dispersive mixing time $H^2/E_l$ is $0.62\ \mathrm{s}$ against an extraction time of the order of a day, so the liquid homogenizes five orders of magnitude faster than tritium leaves it;
    \item The bubble residence time $H/v_b=0.35\ \mathrm{s}$ is more than five orders of magnitude shorter than $\tau$ (\cref{as:short_res_time}), which justifies both the quasi-steady gas phase and the neglect of the gas inventory in front of the liquid one;
    \item The gas Bodenstein number is $\mathrm{Bo}=4.7\times10^{3}$, the ratio of the dispersive to the advective term is $1/\mathrm{Bo}\simeq2\times10^{-4}$ so gas dispersion is negligible (\cref{as:no_gas_dispersion})
    \item The hydrodynamic parameters are close to uniform (\cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic}): the hydrostatic head over the column is $1.5\ \%$ of the pressure at the top, this dictates the order of the variation of $d_b$, $\varepsilon_g$, $a$ and $u_g$ along $z$.
\end{enumerate}

The two regimes are clearly distinguished: at $\langle\Pi\rangle\ll1$ the bubbles stay far from equilibrium and \PT grows almost linearly with height, whereas at $\langle\Pi\rangle\simeq1$ the saturation of the bubbles bends the profile. \Cref{fig:verification_inventory} compares the decay of the tritium inventory with the exponential law of \cref{eq:tau_ana}. The residual grows linearly with time, as expected of a small error on a decay rate, and reaches $1.2\ \%$ after five extraction times in the SPP case. This residual is numerical, not physical: replacing $\tau$ by $\tau+\Delta t/2$, the decay time that a backward Euler scheme actually reproduces (\cref{eq:tau_delta_expansion}), brings the deviation down to $1.2\times10^{-4}$ over the same interval, a hundredfold reduction. The reason why this deviation is more visible in the PPL case is simply because an error on \PI will propagate all the more in \Tana the higher \PI is, since $\Tana=\tau_\mathrm{SPP}\frac{\PI}{1-e^{-\PI}}$ (\cref{eq:tau_ana}).

This verifies the numerical implementation in both the partial pressure regimes and for both the gas phase profile and the integral quantity of interest, down to the discretization error quantified in \cref{sec:numerical_error}.

\chapter{General sparging system design}
\label{sec:sparging_expl}

This chapter uses the ARD model to explore the performance and behavior of the different gas sparging systems that can be designed. As a byproduct of this study, quantified conditions are derived that delimit the validity of the analytical ARD solution, informing the designer if the numerical ARD model is needed. 

% It was obtained by approximating the liquid phase as a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) and approximating a perfect plug-flow for the gas.

\section{Design of the study}
The derivation of the analytical solution \Tana (\cref{eq:tau_ana}) was insightful: \Tana itself is a foundational expression that reveals the parameters that affect sparger performance under certain conditions; also, by requiring some assumptions to be made, \textbf{its derivation reveals what parameters can be varied to relax those assumptions and methodically explore the entire design space of sparging systems}. Indeed, the numerical model does not make such assumptions, and can therefore be compared against the analytical model to: (1) characterize the conditions under which the analytical solution is valid; (2) investigate the change of performance when sparging systems are in other regions of the design space. 

\paragraph{Reduction of the design space to dimensionless groups}

\Tana itself and each assumption made when deriving it in \cref{sec:analytical_solution} can be characterized by a dimensionless group:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \Tana: characterized by the saturation number \PI, in that $\Tana=\tau_\mathrm{SPP}\frac{\PI}{1-e^{-\PI}}$ (\cref{eq:tau_ana})
    \item \cref{as:c_is_uniform} "\CT is uniform": characterized by the ratio of liquid mixing timescale to extraction timescale: 
    \begin{equation}
        \boxed{\Gmix\equiv\frac{\sfrac{H^2}{E_l}}{\tau}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \cref{as:short_res_time} "the residence time of bubbles is negligible": characterized by $\frac{\sfrac{H}{v_b}}{\tau}$
    \item \cref{as:no_gas_dispersion} "Gas dispersion is negligible in front of advection": characterized by the ratio of convective to dispersion transport, namely the Bodenstein number: $\mathrm{Bo}\equiv \frac{\sfrac{H^2}{E_g}}{\sfrac{H}{v_b}}=\frac{H\,v_b}{E_g}$
    \item \cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic} "Hydrodynamic parameters are constant along tank height": characterized by the pressure change ratio across the tank: 
    \begin{equation}
        \boxed{\Gp \equiv \frac{P_\mathrm{bot}-P_\mathrm{top}}{P_\mathrm{top}}=\frac{\rho_l\,g\,H}{P_\mathrm{top}}}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

(disclaimer: it is counter-intuitive that the "mixing" number \Gmix decreases as liquid mixing effects gain importance ($E_l\searrow\Rightarrow\Gmix\nearrow$). In that sense, it can be more intuitive to qualify it as the "\textbf{no-mixing}" number. Still, the \Gmix notation was kept for simplicity)

\paragraph{Excluded from the study}
The bubble speed is expected to stay within $0.2$-$0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}$ no matter its size \cite{chavez_experimental_2021}. Considering that gas sparging columns are of the order of few meters high at most and that the gas dispersion coefficient in the LIBRA Pi experiment $E_g\propto d_t^2\,u_g$ sits around $10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$, we get in most extreme designs: $\frac{H}{v_b}=\frac{10\ \mathrm{m}}{0.2 \ \mathrm{m/s}}=50\ \mathrm{s}\ll \tau$ and $\mathrm{Bo}=\frac{(0.1 \ \mathrm{m}\cdot 0.2 \ \mathrm{m/s})}{(10^{-6} \ \mathrm{m^2/s})\cdot 10^{2}}=200\gg 1$. We can therefore confidently state that \cref{as:short_res_time,as:no_gas_dispersion} are always verified, studying their effect is therefore excluded from this study. 


\paragraph{Included in the study}

\Gmix sits around $10^{-3} \ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ for LIBRA Pi, with $E_l\propto d_t^{1.4}\,u_g^{0.3} \approx 10^{-2} \ \mathrm{m^2/s}$. Assuming a 20-fold decrease of the diameter (a thin and tall column of aspect ratio 1:100) the liquid mixing timescale becomes $\tau_\mathrm{mix}=\frac{(1 \ \mathrm{m})^2}{(10^{-2} \ \mathrm{m^2/s})\cdot0.05^{1.4}}\approx 2\cdot10^3 \ \mathrm{s}$ which might be comparable to very optimistic extraction timescales. It follows that non-uniformity of the liquid concentration could be significant is some designs and should therefore be studied.  

As for \Gp, it is 15\% in LIBRA Pi and can be much more in some designs, e.g. a tall 5 m column would lead to a 100\% increase in pressure as bubbles rise. We can also consider the possibility of pulling vacuum at the top head space (i.e. set \Ptop<1 atm) and investigate the effect the induced bubble decompression will have on extraction kinetics. 

Even though no assumptions were made related to \PI, it appears as a fundamental parameter that directly drives the sparger performance. In particular, high \PI cases are expected to reveal behaviors not predicted by the analytical solution, when associated with a high \Gmix and/or a high \Gp. Looking at its definition (\cref{eq:Pi_def_micro}), \PI is a function of design decisions ($H$) as well as operating conditions ($T$, $d_b$, itself driven by sparger gas flow rate and nozzle diameter as well as tank pressure and height). 

\paragraph{Sampling scheme}
Non-uniformity on \CT and hydrodynamic parameters and their impact on the extraction time are investigated through \Gmix, \Gp and \PI. These 3 dimensionless groups can be controlled by varying any parameter that appears in their definition, but the varied parameters should be independent from each other, so that the effect of each dimensionless group can be isolated and quantified. In this study, we vary $K_s$ (for \PI), $E_l$ (for \Gmix) and $P_\mathrm{top}$ (for \Gp). As can be seen in \cref{fig:closure_graph}, $K_s$ is independent; $E_l$ is linked to $P_\mathrm{top}$ by $E_l\propto u_g^{0.3}$ which is weak enough not to have a significant correlation between \Gmix and \Gp in the study. 


The numerical ARD model being computationally inexpensive, it is especially suited for a Monte Carlo-like approach. 300 cases were simulated, starting from the LIBRA Pi input (\cref{tab:model_input}) and varying the above mentioned parameters by a random multiplier in a log-uniform distribution, chosen broad enough to include all plausible cases of sparging systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K_{s,\mathrm{sample}}=K_{s,\mathrm{LIBRA Pi}}\cdot10^{\mathcal{U}(-2,2)}$
    \item $E_{l,\mathrm{sample}}=E_{l,\mathrm{LIBRA Pi}}\cdot10^{\mathcal{U}(-6,0)}$
    \item $P_{\mathrm{top},\mathrm{sample}}=P_{\mathrm{top},\mathrm{LIBRA Pi}}\cdot10^{\mathcal{U}(-2,2)}$
    \item All other ARD inputs = unchanged from nominal LIBRA Pi case
\end{itemize}
% addressing the second question presented in \cref{sec:problem_def}

Each simulation was run over a time $2\cdot\Tana$ and the resulting \NT curve was fitted with an exponential function to obtain the extraction time \Tnum estimated by the numerical ARD model. The discretization bias of $\sfrac{dt}{2}$ mentioned in \cref{sec:numerical_error} is then subtracted from \Tnum to ease the comparison with \Tana. 
The signed relative error $e=\frac{\Tnum-\Tana}{\Tana}$ gives information about whether the analytical model over/under-estimates performance, it can be plotted on a symmetric logarithmic y-scale to handle negative values. 


\section{Results}

\subsection{Impact of bubbles saturation}
\label{sec:impact_of_sat_Pi}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{fig/regimes_tau_vs_Pi.pdf}
  \caption{Extraction time as a function of bubble saturation \PI and asymptotic behavior, for samples where \Gmix<0.1 and \Gp<0.1}
  \label{fig:tau_vs_Pi}
\end{figure}

Bubble saturation regimes are characterized by \PI, defined in \cref{eq:Pi_def_macro}. Two asymptotic regimes can be distinguished from \cref{fig:tau_vs_Pi}: 

\paragraph{Mass transfer limited regime (MTL)}
When $\PI\ll1 \Rightarrow 1-e^{-\PI}\approx0$: bubbles can be considered free of any tritium, the driving force for tritium exchange \JT is therefore maximal, limited only by the liquid side mass transfer $h_l$, i.e. the ability of tritium to diffuse down the concentration gradient at the liquid-gas interface: this is a \textbf{mass transfer limited (MTL)} regime, which can be characterized by a lower threshold \textbf{\PI<0.1} (\cref{fig:tau_vs_Pi}). In this regime, the extraction time is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:tau_MTL} \boxed{\tau_\mathrm{MTL}=\tau_\mathrm{SPP}=\frac{\langle\varepsilon_l\rangle}{\langle a \, h_l\rangle}}
\end{equation}
It is coupled to tritium diffusivity in the liquid phase through $h_l$ and \textbf{independent of tritium solubility $K_s$}. Among the operating parameters that drive $\tau_\mathrm{MTL}$, temperature is expected to have the greatest impact as it is exponentially correlated to the tritium diffusivity $D_l$ and the liquid viscosity $\mu_l$ by Arrhenius laws (see \cref{tab:correlations}), both governing $h_l$. Therefore, \textbf{increasing temperature greatly improves the extraction performances in MTL}. Increasing the sparging gas flow rate \ndotg also helps, by maximizing the available interfacial area for mass transfer ($a$). 

(Note on semantic: MTL is a regime, SPP is an approximation first made in \cref{sec:extraction_kinetics}. It turns out the SPP approximation is valid under the \PI<0.1 condition, i.e. in the MTL regime)

\paragraph{Partial pressure limited regime (PPL)}
When $\PI\gg1 \Rightarrow1-e^{-\Pi(z)}\approx1$: bubbles leave the tank saturated with tritium, partial pressure approaches equilibrium with the liquid phase ($\PT \rightarrow \frac{\CT}{K_s}$) and the interfacial flux \JT drops as there is no concentration gradient at the liquid-gas interface: it is a partial pressure limited (PPL) regime, graphically characterized by an upper threshold \textbf{\PI>5} (\cref{fig:tau_vs_Pi}).

In this regime, the extraction time follows $\tau\approx \tau_\mathrm{SPP}\cdot\PI \propto \frac{1}{a\,h_l}\cdot \frac{a\,h_l\,K_s\,H}{u_g}\propto \frac{K_s\,H}{u_g}$. More precisely, we can in this case get an exact and compact expression from the general form of \Tana, which doesn't need the "uniform hydrodynamic parameters" assumption: 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:tau_PPL}
    \boxed{\tau_\mathrm{PPL}=\frac{\langle\varepsilon_l\rangle R\,T\,K_s\,H}{u_g(H)}}
\end{equation}

The mass transfer process is throttled by the gas throughput at the outlet, \textbf{extraction time increases with tritium solubility and becomes independent of tritium diffusivity}. Here, temperature primarily drives $K_s$ (exponentially, see \cref{tab:correlations}), thus \textbf{increasing $T$ is counter-productive in PPL}. \textbf{Extraction performances is mainly driven by the sparging gas flow rate \ndotg}, mediated by $u_g$. 

\begin{keyresult}
    \textbf{\PI  conditions the extraction power of individual bubbles}:
    \begin{align*}
    &\PI<0.1 \ \text{(MTL)} \Rightarrow \text{most efficient extraction, high $T$ desirable} \\
    &\PI>5 \ \text{(PPL)} \Rightarrow \text{extraction throttled by saturation, need low $T$ and high \ndotg} \\
    &0.1<\PI<5 \Rightarrow \text{sub-optimal extraction, operating parameters compete with each other}
    \end{align*}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Impact of mixing}
\label{sec:impact_of_mixing}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/validity_error_vs_groups_nodisc.pdf}
  \caption{Signed error of the analytical \Tana against the reference \Tnum, as a function of (a) the mixing number \Gmix and (b) the saturation number \PI. Opaque markers are the samples for which the two groups not on the horizontal axis are both below their own threshold, so the trend against the plotted group is not polluted by them. Red edges mark samples whose inventory decay is not a single exponential, so that \Tnum stops summarising it.}
  \label{fig:error_vs_groups}
\end{figure}

None of the quantities that appear in the expression of \Tana depend on the liquid dispersion coefficient, it is therefore not expected to impact the extraction time. But \textit{is there a point where non-uniform mixing starts affecting extraction time, and if so where is that point ?}

Mixing is characterized by $\Gmix\equiv\frac{\sfrac{H^2}{E_l}}{\tau}$, in this definition the uniformity of \CT degrades with growing \Gmix. As shown in \cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(a), large \Gmix alone isn't sufficient to impact \Tnum: all the samples where the other analytical assumptions hold sit on the zero error line, even at $\Gmix=10^2$ where mixing is much slower than the extraction time hence the liquid concentration can't be considered uniform at all. This suggests that \textbf{the analytical model \Tana might hold even when relaxing the "good liquid mixing" assumption}.

Toward high \Gmix however, many samples exhibit a normalized Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the fit indicating that the inventory decay is no longer well fitted by an exponential. Plotting the same quantity as a function of bubble saturation (\cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(b)) gives us the dimension needed to understand what happens in the high \Gmix region of \cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(a): when mixing becomes insufficient (large \Gmix, indicated by the imperfect fit) and bubbles are saturated (large \PI), extraction kinetics are faster than \Tana, as shown by the systematically overestimated extraction time. Conversely, lack of mixing seems to slow down extraction in the MTL regime compared to the analytical reference, as shown by the positive relative error of the high \Gmix samples in the upper left part of \cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(b). From \cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(a), \textbf{\Gmix<0.1 seems to be a sufficient condition to ensure that \Tnum is obtained from a fit that is representative of the sparger performance}. 


This non-exponential behavior can be explained by examining one of these high-RMSE cases: sample 191 plotted in \cref{fig:nonexp}(b)(poor mixing, bubbles saturated) exhibits a tritium inventory decay that is faster than exponential (normalized RMSE = $2.7\cdot10^{-2}$), suggesting that in this case the characteristic extraction time decreases with time. Other samples from this study exhibit high \Gmix comparable to sample 191 and still follow an exponential decay that is well predicted by \Tana: sample 139, with \Gmix=25, \Gp=0.032, \PI=0.095, differs only significantly by its \PI, it is well fitted (normalized RMSE = $2.7\cdot10^{-5}$) and gives a good prediction ($e=4.3\cdot10^{-3}$). This confirms that \textbf{the non-exponential nature of sample 191 comes from the high \PI}: bubbles saturate soon after being generated, as shown by the spike in the reaction rate $a\,\JT$ in \cref{fig:nonexp}(a), moving up the column as the lower part gets stripped of its tritium. This sparging system behaves like a plug-flow contactor (PFC). At the end of the simulated time, the liquid phase is near fully depleted of its tritium ($\NT(2\Tana)\approx2\%\cdot\NT(0)$), where there should still be 13\% tritium remaining according to the "perfect mixing" result: the extraction is faster than predicted, suggesting that \textbf{poor liquid mixing enhances extraction when operating in a saturated (PPL) regime}. This matches the classic chemical-engineering results that plug-flow contactors beat stirred tank (perfect mixing) whenever the process is equilibrium limited\cite{mohan_experimental_2010}. 

Note that the error of \Tana grows toward the $\PI>1$ region of \cref{fig:error_vs_groups}(b), even when the analytical assumptions are supposed to be valid. This is because \Tana scales linearly with it in the PPL regime, and therefore acts as an error amplifier: if the analytical assumptions are not perfectly valid, \Tana will be even more off as \PI is large. This is the same reason why the error of the verification case was higher in the PPL regime (\cref{fig:verification_inventory}). Here $\Gmix<0.1$ and $\Gp<0.1$ are taken as acceptable indicators of the assumption's validity, still they can be refined to restrict the analytical solution to a higher level of accuracy.  

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/nonexp.pdf}
  \caption{Sample 191 of the study, with \Gmix=25, \Gp=0.032 and \PI=16: (a) the concentration, partial pressure and volumetric transfer rate profiles at five instants, showing a mass transfer front travelling up the column; (b) the decay of the tritium inventory with its exponential fit, and the residual that fit leaves, which is where the departure from a single exponential is visible.}
  \label{fig:nonexp}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/stratified.pdf}
  \caption{Sample 288 of the study, with \Gmix=$1.2\cdot10^{3}$, \Gp=14.5 and \PI=0.017: (a) the concentration, partial pressure and volumetric transfer rate profiles at three instants, showing the liquid stratifying until $a\,\JT$ is nearly uniform along the column. In the partial pressure panel the two analytical profiles are overlaid, the compact one of \cref{eq:PT_profile} and the general one of \cref{eq:PT_profile_gen} which keeps the gas expansion; (b) the decay of the tritium inventory with its exponential fit and with the analytical prediction \Tana, which the fitted decay is $12.4\ \%$ slower than.}
  \label{fig:stratified}
\end{figure}

The opposite situation, where poor mixing makes \Tana too optimistic is illustrated by sample 288 (\cref{fig:stratified}), which combines a badly mixed liquid ($\Gmix=1.2\cdot10^{3}$) with a strong hydrostatic head ($\Gp=14.5$) while the bubbles stay far from saturation ($\PI=0.017$). Because \Gp is large, the sparging gas expands on its way up: the superficial velocity $u_g$ grows by a factor 15 between the sparger and the free surface and the volumetric transfer coefficient $a\,h_l$ by a factor 4.6, so the tritium is stripped mostly from the top of the column. Mixing being far too slow to redistribute it, the liquid stratifies: \CT decreases with height (\cref{fig:stratified}(a)), and the profiles settle into a shape that minimizes $a\,\JT$, the liquid being depleted exactly where the transfer coefficient is largest. The extraction is then slower than \Tana predicts, because the analytical solution evaluates the transfer with a uniform $\langle\CT\rangle^l$ while the actual $a\,h_l$ and \CT oppose each other: \textbf{$\int_0^H a\,h_l\,\CT\,dz$ and $\langle a\,h_l\rangle\,\langle\CT\rangle^l\,H$ are significantly different here}.

The partial pressure panel of \cref{fig:stratified} separates the two assumptions that are violated here. \Cref{eq:PT_profile}, which assumes uniform hydrodynamics, cannot reproduce the profile at all: it is monotonic by construction, whereas the simulated \PT peaks at mid-height and then falls, the expanding gas diluting the tritium faster than the interface supplies it. The general form of \cref{eq:PT_profile_gen}, which keeps $u_g(z)$, follows the simulation to about $1\ \%$ of the peak at $t=0.05\,\Tana$ and only departs from it later, once the liquid has stratified enough that $\langle\CT\rangle^l$ no longer represents the concentration the bubbles actually see. \textbf{The hydrostatic head is therefore what breaks the gas profile, and poor mixing is what turns that into an error on the extraction time.}

\begin{keyresult}
    \textbf{\Gmix  conditions the accuracy of the analytical solution}:
    \begin{align*}
    &\Gmix<0.1 \Rightarrow \text{exponential decay} \\
    &\Gmix>0.1 \ \text{AND} \ \big(\PI>0.1 \ \text{OR} \ \Gp>0.1\big)\Rightarrow \text{non-exponential decay} \\
    &\qquad \text{case} \ \PI<0.1 \ \text{AND} \ \Gp>0.1 \Rightarrow \Tnum>\Tana \ \text{(slower extraction)}\\ 
    &\qquad \text{case} \ \PI>0.1 \Rightarrow \Tnum<\Tana \ \text{(faster extraction, PFC)} \\
    &\PI<0.1 \ \text{AND} \ \Gp<0.1 \Rightarrow \text{\Tana is accurate (even if $\Gmix\gg0.1$)}
    \end{align*}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Impact of pressure variation}
% plot signed error: at higher Gp, tau_pred always overestimates -> the bubble expansion improves the mass transfer properties 
\label{sec:impact_of_pressure}


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/validity_error_vs_GP_nodisc.pdf}
  \caption{Comparison of the signed error on (a) the \Tana obtained when approximating the hydrodynamic conditions everywhere to be the ones evaluated at the bottom of the tank; (b) the \Tana evaluated from spatially averaged hydrodynamic parameters}
  \label{fig:error_vs_Gp}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{fig/design_tau_vs_Ptop.pdf}
  \caption{Correlation of the extraction time with the head space pressure when operating in the SPP regime. The shaded region corresponds to samples where the gas fraction at the top of the tank exceeds 1\%, challenging the "no-bubble coalescence" assumption}
  \label{fig:tau_vs_Ptop}
\end{figure}


Pressure variation is characterized by $\Gp\equiv \frac{\rho_l\,g\,H}{\Ptop}$. The comparison between \cref{fig:error_vs_Gp}(a) and (b) shows the importance of taking into account hydrostatic pressure variations when modelling gas sparging: even at relatively small $\Gp=0.2$ (typically LIBRA Pi case), ignoring pressure variations can result in a 5\% overestimation extraction time, which gets worse at higher \Gp. Despite of yielding a conservative estimate, this represents added error which is always undesirable. 

As shown in \cref{fig:error_vs_Gp}(b), using spatially averaged quantities as derived in \cref{sec:analytical_solution} yields an analytical prediction that matches well the numerical results when the underlying assumptions are valid. Interestingly, it can be seen that around $\Gp> 5$, the analytical model starts to break down and overestimates the extraction time. This is a hint that bubble's expansion is beneficial to mass transfer. 

To look at it more closely, we can isolate the impact of pressure on the extraction time by considering only the small \PI samples, as shown in \cref{fig:tau_vs_Ptop}. Here, the SPP approximation is valid and $\tau$ becomes only a function of $\langle a\,h_l\rangle$ and $\langle\varepsilon_l\rangle$ (\cref{eq:tau_SPP}), which neither $K_s$ nor $E_l$ vary. This allows to isolate the effect of varying $P_\mathrm{top}$, to reveal a clear positive correlation shown in \cref{fig:tau_vs_Ptop}: $\tau\propto P_\mathrm{top}^{0.50}$. This suggests that \textbf{pumping the top head space to a low pressure could be used as a mechanism to accelerate tritium extraction}, at least when operating in the SPP regime (the $\PI>0.1$ samples also seem to follow this trend, although less clearly). This might however hit a limit when bubble's expansion make them coalesce, which reduces the specific interfacial area $a$ but is not caught by this model, as per \cref{as:no_coalescence}.

At the same gas flow rate and assuming no bubble coalescence, the observed effect of pressure is explained as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item in MTL regime: extraction is driven by the specific bubble interfacial area and the mass transfer: $\tau \propto a^{-1}h_l^{-1}$ (\cref{eq:tau_MTL}), with $a\propto \varepsilon_g \,d_b^{-1}$ and $h_l \propto d_b^{-1/3}$. Given $\epsilon_g\propto P_l^{-1}$ whereas $d_b\propto P_l^{-1/3}$, this yields $\tau\propto P_l^{5/9}$, close to the observed tendency;
    \item in PPL regime: extraction is throttled by the bubbles back-pressure \PT. Intuitively, a lower initial pressure or a decompression of the bubble as it rises allows to accommodate more tritium in the bubble. Formally, the extraction time derived for the PPL regime suggests $\tau\propto P_l$ (\cref{eq:tau_PPL}).
\end{itemize}
Here, \textbf{for a given gas molar flow rate}, pressure drives variations of the gas volume, competing with variations of the size of individual bubbles. 
This doesn't contradict the well-established result that \textbf{for a given gas volume}, smaller bubbles are desirable to maximize the interfacial area available for mass transfer, this is controlled by the design of the nozzles.  

\begin{keyresult}
    \centering
    Hydrostatic pressure variation must not be ignored when \Gp>0.1 \\
    \textbf{Lower head space pressure $\Rightarrow$ faster extraction}
\end{keyresult}

\paragraph{When can one use the analytical model ?}
 Defining the threshold tolerance at $\pm5\%$, the parity plot of \cref{fig:parity} shows that \textbf{having no more than one the three assumptions violated is a sufficient condition for the analytical model to hold}: all 111 samples satisfying it fall within the reference $\Tnum\pm5\%$, the largest deviation being 3.3\%. It does not matter which of the three is the one relaxed, so this contains in particular the insight that the "good-mixing" assumption can be relaxed ($\Gmix \nless 0.1$) and the analytical model still hold, as found in \cref{sec:impact_of_mixing}. Beyond that the inaccuracy of the analytical results is abrupt, as \cref{fig:venn} shows region by region: as soon as a second assumption is violated the deviation can reach 41\%, and up to 46\% when all three are.

\paragraph{How to optimize the sparging system's design}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/design_violin.pdf}
  \caption{Extraction time reached across the design space, normalized by $\tau_\mathrm{ref}$, the median extraction time of case 1. The 8 cases of \cref{tab:design_map} are paired by their $(\PI,\Gp)$ combination and ranked from fastest to slowest, the two members of each pair differing only in \Gmix.}
  \label{fig:violin}
\end{figure}


In a design problem, the tritium transport properties are not controlled directly. \Cref{fig:violin} compares what each region of the design space can reach, and the spread within a case there reflects the sampled transport parameters, so it is the medians that compare the regimes. The designer controls the tank geometry $H$ and $d_t$, the sparger nozzles and the range of expected operational parameters $T$, \ndotg and \Ptop, all of which are synthesized in the dimensionless groups \PI, \Gp and \Gmix.

The optimal design depends on the constraints: beside the obvious extraction time goal, there might be cases when the tritium concentration of the outlet gas becomes a strong constraint. For instance in the LIBRA Pi experiment, after being extracted from the vessel, tritium is stripped from its carrying gas by water bubblers (ready to be counted using a liquid scintillator). The gas rate drives evaporation in those bubblers, it is thus desirable that the tritium concentration in the gas is optimized so that the gas flow rate itself can be kept low enough, eventually enabling a higher tritium concentration in the water samples to be counted. 

A ranking of the relative performance in each region of the design space is shown in \cref{fig:violin}, the fastest on the left. It reveals \Gp as being the primary lever for extraction performance, Within each half, the \PI<0.1 case comes first, with a factor $\sim 3$ improvement compared to the \PI>0.1 case. \Gmix on the other hand doesn't seem to significantly change extraction performance (cases 7 and 3 are barely distinguishable, as are 5 and 1). 

This answers the design optimization problem:
\begin{itemize}
    \item  case \textit{optimize for the extraction time}: the system should operate in MTL regime (regions 3 or 7 of the design space); \Gp maximized by pulling vacuum on the head space ($\Ptop \searrow$) and making the column taller (while still ensuring \PI<0.1); mixing irrelevant (no constraint on \Gmix). During operation, the extraction time can be reduced by increasing $T$ and \ndotg and decreasing \Ptop;
    \item case \textit{optimize for tritium concentration in the outlet gas}: the system has to operate in PPL regime, at the expense of slower extraction (region 4 and 8 of the design space); even though slower, extraction performance can still be optimized by pulling vacuum in the head space (high \Gp). When operating in PPL, the extraction time is reduced by increasing \ndotg and decreasing \Ptop, increasing $T$ can still have a positive effect if the bubbles are not fully saturated (\PI<5), but if they are it starts having an adverse effect.
\end{itemize}

The model input corresponding to the LIBRA Pi experiment is reported in \cref{tab:model_input}. The quarter-annulus geometry of LIBRA Pi (see \cref{fig:librapi}) containing a salt volume of $\sim120$ L is described by a cylinder of height 0.93 m and equivalent base area $A=0.13 \ \mathrm{m^2}$ chosen to conserve salt volume. 

LIBRA Pi will run in ClLiF molten salt, which is a convenient experimental breeder as it doesn't have Be toxicity problem. The current state of literature on ClLiF doesn't contain correlations for its tritium transport properties. This last point motivates the choice of rather using the properties of other molten salts as a ClLiF proxy. The physical properties (density, surface tension, viscosity) were taken for \ce{FLiBe}, they are expected to have a secondary impact on kinetics prediction. Regarding the tritium transport properties, this makes the extraction time estimate inherently uncertain. Then, the best that can be done is to enclose the extraction time estimate between upper and lower bounds, corresponding to pessimistic and optimistic scenarios derived from the currently available correlations for tritium transport in molten salt (presented in \cref{fig:transport_params}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{pessimistic scenario} = high solubility (i.e. the salt wants to retain tritium) and low diffusivity (slowing down mass transfer on the liquid side of the liquid-gas interface): $K_s$ and $D_l$ are both given by the correlations derived by \textcite{calderoni_measurement_2008} for \ce{T2} in \ce{FLiBe};
    \item \textit{optimistic scenario} = low solubility and high diffusivity: $K_s$ is given by the \textcite{malinauskas_solubilities_1974} correlation for \ce{D_2} in \ce{FLiBe}, $D_l$ is given by the \textcite{fukada_hydrogen_2006} correlation for \ce{H2} in \ce{FLiNaK}. 
\end{itemize}

It should be noted that those correlations were derived for temperatures greater than 550°C, the temperature range we consider here goes below that. Nevertheless extrapolating these correlations constitutes the broadest upper/lower bounds that can be derived, ensuring the actual extraction time is well enclosed between the pessimistic and optimistic estimates. 

The sparger design isn't decided yet. The number of nozzles in particular dictates the gas volumetric flow rate through each nozzle for a given total mass flow rate. In this work, it is assumed that the sparger is made of 4 nozzles, so that the volumetric flow rate of each nozzle in most of the operating conditions stays within $3-10\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}$, which is the validity range of the correlation used for the initial bubble diameter\cite{kanai_establishment_2017}. 

Beside the already fixed geometry, only four quantities can be controlled by the experiment: the temperature ($T$), the gas molar flow rate (\ndotg), the head space pressure (\Ptop) and the sparger nozzle diameter (\dnoz). Their range is reported in \cref{tab:model_input}. Everything else comes from the closure relations of \cref{tab:correlations,tab:correlations_hydro}. 

The initial tritium inventory is fixed to $3.6\cdot10^{-12} \ \mathrm{molT2}$ (equivalent to $\langle\CT\rangle =3.0\cdot10^{-11} \ \mathrm{molT2/m^3}$) to correspond to an irradiation of $2\cdot10^{9} \ \mathrm{n/s}$ (the available 14 MeV neutron source) for 6 hours and a TBR=0.1 (calculated for the LIBRA Pi geometry using OpenMC). That being said, in the case of the sparging model itself, as long as the "negligible contribution of \PT to bubble pressure" (\cref{as:T2_press_is_negligible}) holds, it could take any value this would not change the extraction time since the ARD equations are linear. This will however become relevant in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_permeation} where the boundary condition for the concentration at the salt-metal interface is non-linear. 



\subsection{Sparging regimes}


Considering the range of attainable operating parameters presented in \cref{tab:model_input}, the LIBRA Pi sparging system is characterized by the following ranges on its dimensionless groups:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Gmix=1.7\times10^{-4}-4.0\times10^{-3}$: the aspect ratio of the tank is such that the salt is well mixed at any operating point. The liquid concentration is uniform and the 0D description of the salt inventory holds;
    \item $\Gp=0.09-0.18$: growth of $a$ and $u_g$ as  gas expands along $z$ is non-negligible. The numerical model will catch this. 
    \item As for $\PI$, it is the uncertain parameter that motivates the 2 scenarios:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item optimistic: $\PI=3.5\cdot10^{-5}-2.4\cdot10^{-3}$, the bubbles are always in the MTL regime
        \item pessimistic: $\PI=0.03-2.2$, spans MTL up to the entrance of the PPL regime. The temperature might therefore have an adverse effect on the extraction in some regions of the operational parameters space. 
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

It follows that LIBRA Pi can potentially be in regions 3 or 4 of \cref{fig:venn}, where the analytical model might underestimates the extraction speed. It must therefore be simulated using the numerical ARD model. As in \cref{sec:sparging_expl}, we use a Monte Carlo-like approach to cover the entire 4-dimensional parameter space for each of the 2 bounding scenarios. The four operating parameters are sampled with a Saltelli design, which is the sampling scheme the Sobol estimator requires: it draws two independent base matrices and builds from them the cross-sampled matrices that isolate the contribution of each input. With $N=256$ base samples and $d=4$ inputs this gives $N(d+2)=1536$ model evaluations per scenario. Temperature, head space pressure and nozzle diameter are drawn uniformly over their attainable range, while the gas flow rate is drawn log-uniformly since it spans a decade. Each sample is run for a transient duration of $5\Tana$, enough to observe the inventory decay below 1\% of its original value.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/sobol_indices.pdf}
  \caption{Sobol sensitivity indices of the tritium removal time $\tau_{99}$ with respect to the four controllable operating parameters, for (a) the pessimistic and (b) the optimistic transport scenario. Whiskers are bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals over 999 resamples.}
  \label{fig:sobol}
\end{figure}

A sensitivity study was run on the 4 operating parameters in their respective range, to reveal: (1) what parameters matter most for the sparging system performance; (2) quantify in what extent uncertainty on an operating parameter propagates to the extraction time. Sensitivity of a function on its variables can be characterized by Sobol indices. It is a variance based sensitivity metric, quantifying the variance that propagates to the output when varying one of its inputs, therefore measuring the sensitivity of a function to each of its individual variables.

In the present case, the sensitivity study in \cref{fig:sobol} shows that 2 input parameters dominate the system's performance, regardless of the scenario: in the considered parameter space, variations in $T$ and \ndotg are responsible for most of the extraction time variance, indicating that the contribution of \Ptop and \dnoz can be ignored to a certain extent. The two orders overlap within their confidence intervals for every parameter, and $\sum_i S_i$ reaches $0.96$ in the pessimistic scenario and $0.94$ in the optimistic one, so \textbf{interactions account for only 4 to 6\% of the variance of $\tau_{99}$} and the response can be read as essentially additive: the effect of each operating parameter can be considered separately from the others. This allows to isolate $T$ and \ndotg as the most important operating parameters, reducing the parameter space from 4D to 2D to simplify the analysis.

It can also be observed that the relative importance of \ndotg and $T$ flips from the pessimistic to the optimistic scenario. This matches the analysis of \cref{sec:impact_of_sat_Pi}, stating that as the extraction starts being limited by tritium partial pressure (high \PI, the pessimistic scenario), sparger gas flow rate becomes the main extraction driver and increasing temperature becomes counter-productive.  

\paragraph{Note on Sobol indices}

Sobol indices decompose the variance of a model output over its inputs. Writing $Y=f(X_1,\dots,X_d)$ for the output and $\mathrm{Var}(Y)$ for its total variance, the first order index of an input measures the part of that variance removed on average by fixing it, $S_i=\mathrm{Var}_{X_i}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[Y|X_i]\right)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$, i.e. what that input explains on its own. The total order index measures instead the variance that remains when every other input is fixed, $S_{T_i}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{Var}(Y|X_{\sim i})\right]/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$, and therefore includes everything the input contributes through its interactions with the others. The two coincide when the model is additive, and $S_{T_i}>S_i$ signals interaction; $\sum_i S_i=1$ for a perfectly additive model and falls below 1 otherwise, the deficit being the share of variance carried by interactions.

It should be noted that the width of the sampling space for each parameter conditions the indices the Sobol study will yield: in a function $f(a,b)=a+e^b$, if $a\in[1,10^5]$ and $b\in[1,1.01]$, $a$ will have the dominating Sobol index even though $f$ depends exponentially on $b$ and only linearly on $a$. The sample space for a simulation must be representative of what is expected to be encountered in the experiment. It is the case here.

\subsection{Limitations}

The largest limitation of this analysis is that ClLiF is modelled with the properties of other fluoride salts: its physical properties are taken from FLiBe and its tritium transport properties bracketed between correlations measured on \ce{FLiBe} and \ce{FLiNaK}. The optimistic and pessimistic scenarios therefore bound a \textit{choice of correlation}, and should not be read as a confidence interval: each correlation carries its own uncertainty and the operating parameters they take value from are known within a limited confidence. The operating map of \cref{fig:abaque} can be used to quickly propagate operational uncertainty graphically:  a $550\pm25$ K uncertainty on the temperature distribution in the salt at the nominal operating point propagates to a $130\pm20$ hours uncertainty on the extraction time in the pessimistic salt scenario. Comparison with the $90\pm40$ hours extraction time interval brought by the uncertainty on the salt properties at the same operating point reveals that \textbf{the salt properties constitute the dominant source of uncertainty}.

The correlation used for the initial bubble diameter \cite{kanai_establishment_2017} was validated for volumetric flow rates of 3 to 10 $\mathrm{cm^3/s}$ per nozzle. Modulo head space pressure variations, the four-nozzle sparger considered here goes below this range when $\ndotg\lesssim 350$ sccm, predictions in the low flow corner of \cref{fig:abaque} are therefore extrapolations of that correlation.

The numbers reported here, even though bracketed between lower and upper bounds, are mere simulation results that haven't been validated. Eventually the operating map (\cref{fig:abaque}) will be challenged against LIBRA Pi experimental data. An approach to validate this map could be to operate at high temperature, to mitigate the propagation of uncertainty on the measured temperature, and only vary the easier-to-measure \ndotg. This would first indicate if the trend that the ARD model suggests is actually observed, and if so which tritium transport scenario (pessimistic/optimistic) ClLiF is closest to.

\chapter{Tritium permeation in LIBRA Pi}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:LIBRAPi_permeation}

% presentation of FESTIM: see file:///C:/Users/hippo/OneDrive/temp/Estimations%20of%20tritium%20retention%20through%20DT1%20in%20a%20full%20tungsten%20ITER.pdf

Tritium is expected to permeate through structural materials in general. This is accounted for in the BABY and LIBRA experiments as one of the removal pathways, competing with other extraction mechanisms such as sparging in LIBRA Pi. As part of the accountancy goal of these experiments, the permeated tritium is collected using a double-walled vessel. However, the design of the LIBRA Pi vessel also opens some structural pathways for tritium to leak to the environment, challenging the robustness of tritium accountancy.

The purpose of this chapter is therefore to evaluate the amount of tritium that permeates and quantify what portion of it leaks out during a run, in light of the sparging extraction dynamics predicted in \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging}.


\section{Tritium transport model}

\subsection{Geometry}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[height=8cm, trim={0 0.5cm 0 1.5cm}, clip]{fig/librapi.pdf}
  \caption{Orthographic projection of (a) the LIBRA Pi experiment viewed from the outside, with labelled faces; (b) an exploded view of the LIBRA Pi double-walled vessel (IV and OV) mechanically coupled by spacer ribs.}
  \label{fig:librapi}
\end{figure}

The quarter-annulus geometry of the LIBRA Pi experiment is shown in \cref{fig:librapi}(a). We are specifically interested about the vessel itself, consisting of an \textit{inner and outer wall} (IV and OV) so that tritium that permeates from the salt to the structural material can be collected by running a sweep gas in-between the two walls, called \textit{OV gas}. The salt volume doesn't fill the vessel all the way up, it stops at a certain height which delimits the portion of the wall that is \textit{wetted} and can permeate. In LIBRA Pi specifically, some spacers are required between the IV and OV to hold the walls together, as pictured in \cref{fig:librapi}(b). They make several parallel ribs spanning the whole width of 5 of the 6 faces: \textit{bottom}, 2 \textit{sides}, \textit{inner} and \textit{outer}. Small repeated archs are carved in the spacers, these are channels that allow the OV gas to flow. They add complexity to the spacer's geometry.  Resolving the small features of the multiple spacers would be intractable in 3D at the scale of the full vessel. Instead, we run the 3D simulations at a smaller scale, from which we reconstruct the vessel-scale quantities of interest.

\subsubsection{Scale reduction methodology}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=7cm, trim={0 8cm 0 1cm}, clip]{fig/cell.pdf}
        \caption{Simulated unit cell and its dimensions: (a-a) perspective front view without the OV wall, showing one spacer rib with evenly spaced gas channels, the dotted-dashed line indicates the cross-section shown in (a-b) the top view. "spacer" is abbreviated as "s"}
        \label{fig:cell}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hspace{1 em}
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=7cm, trim={0 2cm 0 2cm}, clip]{fig/BCs.pdf}
        \caption{Clipped view of the unit cell with visible HEX elements (medium refinement), bulk volume is shown transparent, revealing labeled surfaces where boundary conditions are applied}
        \label{fig:Bcs}
    \end{subfigure}
\end{figure}


The 3D transport simulations are run at the scale of a unit cell that repeats periodically on the vessel, described in \cref{fig:cell}. The 4 faces types listed in \cref{fig:librapi}(a) have slightly different spacer periodicity, this require individual treatments. For each face, the dimensions tight to the spacers are given by the spacer geometry itself; regarding the cell's dimensions, $L_\mathbf{cell}=L_\mathbf{s}+L_\mathbf{gap}$ is fixed by the periodicity of the spacer channels and $w_\mathrm{cell}=w_\mathrm{IV}+w_\mathrm{s}+w_\mathrm{OV}$ is the actual thickness of the vessel, so the only degree of freedom is $H_\mathrm{cell}$. The cell-scale$\rightarrow$vessel-scale reconstruction works as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It is assumed that the fraction of permeated tritium that leaks out is dictated by the contact surface area of spacers with wetted walls $A_{\mathrm{contact}}$, since it offers a pathway for leakage. The unit cell should therefore be representative of it. We define the contact surface ratio of a geometrical entity $i$ (a \textit{cell} or a \textit{face}) as:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:chi_def}
        \chi_i\equiv\frac{A_{\mathrm{contact},i}}{A_{\mathrm{total},i}}
    \end{equation}
    It is important that ${A_{\mathrm{total,face}}}$ is the area that is \textbf{wetted} since it is only this surface that permeates. Similarly, the spacer rows that is above the salt limit shouldn't be accounted for in ${A_{\mathrm{contact,face}}}$.
    \item For the results obtained with one unit cell to be applicable to a whole face of the vessel, we want: $ \chi_\mathrm{face}=\chi_\mathrm{cell}=\frac{L_\mathrm{s}\,H_\mathrm{s}}{L_\mathrm{cell}\,H_\mathrm{cell}}$. This fixes $H_\mathrm{cell}=\frac{L_\mathrm{s}\,H_\mathrm{s}}{L_\mathrm{cell}\,\chi_\mathrm{face}}$;
    \item The contact surface ratio $\chi_\mathrm{face}$ for each face is obtained by simple application of its definition in \cref{eq:chi_def} (taking into account the void left by the spacers channels), yielding for the 4 different faces types: $\chi_\mathrm{bottom}=6.8\%$, $\chi_\mathrm{side}=4.6\%$, $\chi_\mathrm{inner}=4.9\%$, $\chi_\mathrm{outer}=5.2\%$;
    \item This prescribes running the transport simulation on \textbf{4 different cell types}, with each respective $\chi_\mathrm{cell}$ matching the $\chi_\mathrm{face}$ of the corresponding face type;
    \item Once results for all the cell types are obtained, any vessel scale quantity of interest $\psi$ can be reconstructed by summing up the contribution of individual cell types $\psi_k$, weighted by a multiplier $m_k$:
    \begin{equation}
        \psi=\sum_{k=1}^4 m_k \psi_k
    \end{equation}
    Note that $\psi$ must be an \textbf{extensive} quantity here (e.g. total permeated tritium, e.g. not a concentration). Intuitively, $m_k$ is chosen so that a reconstructed face has the same total surface area as its original face; the contact surface ratio being conserved it will also have the same contact surface area. Formally, we enforce:
    \begin{align}
        \left.
        \begin{array}{rcl}
        &A_{\mathrm{total, reconstructed}\,k} &= m_k \,A_{\mathrm{total, cell}\,k}\\
        &A_{\mathrm{total, reconstructed}\,k} &= A_{\mathrm{total, face}\,k}
        \end{array}\right\} \Rightarrow m_k \equiv \frac{A_{\mathrm{total, face}\,k}}{A_{\mathrm{total, cell}\,k}}
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

Under these definitions, the spacer contact surface of each reconstructed face is indeed conserved from the original face:
\begin{align*}
    A_{\mathrm{contact, reconstructed}\,k} &= m_k \,A_{\mathrm{contact, cell}\,k}\\
    &=A_{\mathrm{total, face}\,k}\,\frac{A_{\mathrm{contact, cell}\,k}}{A_{\mathrm{total, cell}\,k}}\\
    &=A_{\mathrm{total, face}\,k}\,\chi_{\mathrm{cell},k}\\
    &=A_{\mathrm{total, face}\,k}\,\chi_{\mathrm{face},k}\\
    &=A_{\mathrm{contact, face}\,k}
\end{align*}

The quantities derived using this scale reduction methodology can therefore be assumed representative of what would have been obtained with a full vessel-scale simulation. The obtained dimensions and multipliers for each cell type are summarized in \cref{tab:unit_cell}. Details about all the dimensions used in this model can be found in the \verb|geometry.py| script of the \verb|LibraPi_transport| repository publicly available on GitHub. They were all measured from the CAD \verb|.step| model of LIBRA Pi with a $\pm0.1$ mm uncertainty.

\subsection{Equations and coupling}

Tritium transport in the inconel is a pure diffusion problem, solved with the open-source hydrogen transport code FESTIM 2.0\cite{dark_festim_2026}. 

What makes the problem specific to LIBRA Pi are the boundary conditions and the bi-directional coupling with the sparging model.

\subsubsection{Boundary conditions}

There are 3 different boundary conditions, one per surface of the unit cell as labeled on \cref{fig:Bcs}. It is useful to read them as \textbf{three resistances in series}: tritium has to cross the salt to reach the wall, diffuse through the metal, and then leave it, either into the OV gas or into the environment:

\paragraph{salt surface BC} 
Molten salts are tritium repellants and metals are tritium sponges. We can't consider the inconel surface concentration to be at equilibrium with the salt, it is physically unrealistic: after irradiation, the $3.6\cdot10^{13} \ \mathrm{T/m^3}$ in the salt becomes $4.0\cdot10^{20}\ \mathrm{T/m^3}$ in the metal if at equilibrium, which after a few timesteps represents a tritium inventory  of $\sim10^{18}$ T atoms in the mere IV wall, i.e. $\sim10^6$ times the total bred tritium actually available. \textbf{The tritium flux to the wall has to be limited by transport on the salt side}. This is enforced by considering a flux BC that accounts for salt transport:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:salt_BC}
    J_\mathrm{salt}=k_\mathrm{eff}\,\left(c_\mathrm{salt}(t)-K_H\,\left(\frac{c_\mathrm{T}(t)}{K_s}\right)^2\right)
\end{equation}
where $k_\mathrm{eff}$ is the salt-to-wall mass transfer coefficient. The second term is the salt concentration that would be in equilibrium with the local metal surface, obtained by inverting Sieverts' law then applying Henry's law. It allows $J_\mathrm{salt}$ reverse sign on its own once the wall is more loaded than the salt, which can happen at the end of a run.


\paragraph{OV gas surface BC}
\ce{T} atoms leave the metal into the OV gas by either: recombining with another \ce{T} into \ce{T2} ($\mathrm{2^{nd}}$ order reaction); recombining with a \ce{H} adsorbed at the OV surface into \ce{HT}; or by isotopic exchange with a partner molecule of the sweep gas (e.g. through \ce{T + H2O -> H + HTO} or \ce{T + H2 -> H + HT}). The first channel is quadratic in the surface concentration, the others are linear, so the flux out of the sweep gas facing surfaces is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sweep_BC}
    J_\mathrm{OV}=-k_r\,c_\mathrm{T}^2-k_e\,c_\mathrm{X}\,c_\mathrm{T}
\end{equation}
with $k_r=4\cdot10^{-28} \ \mathrm{m^4/(T\,s)}$ for tritium in a clean (non-oxidized) inconel 625 surface at 550°C\cite{perujo_low_1996}. Three sweep gas compositions are considered, an inert carrier alone and the same carrier with one of two reactive partners added:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{He only}: an inert carrier, so $k_e\,c_\mathrm{X}=0$ and recombination is the only channel;
    \item \textbf{He + 3.5\% \ce{H2}}: $\mathrm{T(metal)}+\mathrm{H(metal, adsorbed)}\rightarrow \mathrm{HT}$, so the same $k_r$ applies and $c_\mathrm{X}$ is the hydrogen the sweep gas loads into the surface, assumed at Sievert's equilibrium with its partial pressure. At 3.5\% \ce{H2} in a 1.2 atm sweep gas, $c_\mathrm{H}=K_s\sqrt{P_\mathrm{H_2}}=8.7\cdot10^{23} \ \mathrm{H/m^3}$, hence $\frac{1}{2}k_r\,c_\mathrm{H}=3.5\cdot10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}$;
    \item \textbf{He + \ce{H2O}}: $\mathrm{T(metal)}+\mathrm{H_2O(gas)}\rightarrow \mathrm{HTO}$, with the product $k_e\,c_\mathrm{H_2O}=4.6\cdot10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, an unpublished estimate obtained by fitting data on previous BABY 1L runs. This single number is what separates a sweep gas that collects tritium from one that does not (\cref{sec:perm_sweep}), so it deserves to be better constrained than it currently is.
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{outside surface BC}
The outer face of the OV wall is open to the experimental room, where tritium is assumed to be immediately carried away by ambient air. It is modelled as a perfect sink, $c_\mathrm{T}=0$. Any surface resistance there would reduce the leakage, so this is the conservative choice.

\paragraph{Salt-side mass transfer}
The mass transfer coefficient $k_m$ for salt transport is obtained from a Chilton-Colburn analogy applied to the bubble column wall correlation of \textcite{deckwer_mechanism_1980}, in which the column diameter cancels and only the superficial gas velocity remains:
\begin{equation}
    k_m=C\,(g\,\nu_l\,u_g)^{1/4}\,\mathrm{Sc}^{-1/2}
\end{equation}
With $C$ a prefactor that can take values from 1.13 (theoretical upper bound) to 0.1 (empirical). The liquid properties are the same as those resolved by the sparging model, so the salt transport correlations of \cref{tab:correlations} are reused here rather than duplicated. At nominal LIBRA Pi conditions this gives $k_m=3.2\cdot10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

Still large uncertainties remain about the salt-to-metal transport, the presence of an oxide layer could change the flux by an order of magnitude. We account for this by considering a permeation reduction factor (PRF) applied to $k_m$ so that the salt BC uses an effective mass transfer coefficient $k_\mathrm{eff}\equiv k_m / \mathrm{PRF}$, and we simulate the four values $\mathrm{PRF}=1, 10, 100, 1000$. The PRF is applied on the salt side only: the oxide grows where the metal meets the salt, while the OV side is swept with an inert or reducing gas. Note that this sweep also covers the uncertainty on the prefactor $C$ of the correlation itself, which is only known to within a factor of about 10: the PRF spans three decades on $k_\mathrm{eff}$, so no plausible $C$ falls outside the range simulated.

\subsubsection{Sparging-permeation coupling}

Permeation will impact the amount of tritium available to be extracted by sparging, and the other way around. The modelling on sparging done in this work allowed to establish that the concentration in the salt can be assumed uniform and that we can use a simple time constant $\tau$, independently of the concentration, to represent sparging extraction. The coupling can simply be achieved through a 0D mass balance:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:salt_balance}
    V_\mathrm{salt}\frac{d\,c_\mathrm{salt}}{dt}=-V_\mathrm{salt}\,\frac{c_\mathrm{salt}}{\tau}-A_\mathrm{wetted}\,J_\mathrm{salt}
\end{equation}

We decided to treat the optimistic scenario of \cref{sec:LIBRAPi_sparging} in nominal conditions, with $\tau_{99}=55.6$ hours. To stay consistent, we use the \textcite{malinauskas_solubilities_1974} and the \textcite{fukada_hydrogen_2006} correlations for the salt solubility and diffusivity respectively.

All the structural material is considered made of inconel. In practice, the salt is expected to reach 550°C and the vessel to be below that. Here, we conservatively consider the vessel is at salt temperature, so that the predicted tritium permeation is an upper bound.

\subsubsection{Numerical scheme}

The transport equation is discretised with linear Lagrange elements on the unit cell mesh and integrated in time with a backward Euler scheme, the resulting non-linear system being solved by a Newton method at each time step; the details of the FESTIM implementation are documented in \textcite{delaporte-mathurin_festim_2024}. Transient simulations are run over 4 days, well above the $\tau_{99}$=55.6 hours sparging extraction time, so that effects of wall tritium inventory diffusing back to the salt can be observed. The time step is ramped geometrically from 30 s to 30 min, which resolves the initial loading of the wall without paying for it over the rest of the 4 days simulated.

The coupling of \cref{eq:salt_balance} is the part specific to this work. The unit cells do not interact with each other, but they all draw from the same salt, so they cannot be run independently: they are stepped \textbf{in lockstep on a common time step}, and after each step the tritium that entered every cell is summed with the multipliers $m_k$ and removed from the 0D salt inventory, alongside what sparging extracted. The balance is integrated semi-implicitly, the sparging term implicitly and the permeation term explicitly, so that the extraction cannot overshoot and drive the inventory negative. The updated $c_\mathrm{salt}$ is then written back into the salt-side BC of every cell before the next step. 

To keep the computational cost reasonable, only 2 of the 4 cell types are simulated: the bottom cell, and the inner cell standing in for the 3 lateral faces. This is justified by $\chi_\mathrm{inner}$ sitting in the middle of the 3 lateral contact ratios (\cref{tab:unit_cell}), and the multiplier of the inner cell is then taken as the one conserving the total lateral wetted area.

\subsection{Mesh independence}

\paragraph{Tested meshes}
Two different meshes are tested for the same geometry:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unstructured tetrahedral mesh (TET): this is the first mesh that was tried, as it is straight forward to create. A strength of using tetrahedral elements is that it is able to reproduce complex shapes, in our case the arched shape of the spacer's channel.
    \item Structured hexagonal mesh (HEX): for redundancy and because the tetrahedral mesh was showing poor numerical convergence performances. A trade-off is that the arched channel has to be approximated by a parallelepipedic one.
\end{itemize}

The parallelepipedic approximation of the channel's shape in the HEX mesh leads to a slight increase of the recombination surface even if the height of the parallelepiped ($w_\mathrm{gap}$) is allowed to vary. Its other dimensions ($H_\mathrm{s}$, $L_\mathrm{gap}$) can't change without corrupting the contact ratio $\chi_\mathrm{s}$ which would cause greater harm to the fidelity of the estimated leakage. It was therefore chosen to tune $w_\mathrm{gap}$ so that the volume of the spacer is conserved between the two meshes.

% safety factor SF=1.25 as recommanded for GCI on 3 or more grids
% we use P1 linear lagrange elements which theoretically give an L2 norm of the error in O(h^2) for the field themselves and O(h) for their gradient. Bounday fluxes are obtained from gradient so the results p=1-1.3 are consistent with the theory

\paragraph{Mesh convergence}

Both meshes are refined over 4 levels (extra coarse, coarse, medium, fine) with a refinement factor of 2. Each refinement is compared against its own Richardson-extrapolated value, i.e. its own mesh-independent limit. The result is shown in \cref{fig:mesh_selection}. The HEX mesh converges monotonically over the whole series, at an observed order $p=0.98$ on the leakage flux and $p=1.31$ on the OV flux. This is consistent with what is expected from linear Lagrange elements: boundary fluxes are obtained from the gradient of the field, which converges one order slower than the field itself. The TET mesh behaves less cleanly: its leakage flux is not monotonic over the 2 coarsest levels, which is the usual signature of a series that has not reached its asymptotic regime, and its OV flux converges at $p=0.50$, well below the expected order. This is not a coincidence: the transfinite grading of the HEX mesh specifically targets the re-entrant corner between the rib and the channel, which is where the discretization error is concentrated, whereas the TET mesh refines isotropically and does not target that region at all.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/mesh_selection.pdf}
  \caption{Mesh selection among the 4 refinement levels of hexahedral (solid) and tetrahedral (dashed) meshes: (a) discretization error on the two permeating fluxes and (b) mass conservation error, both against the number of degrees of freedom. The error is measured against each mesh type's own Richardson-extrapolated value, denoted $f_\mathrm{exact}$. The dotted horizontal line is the leakage flux bias introduced by the parallelepipedic channel of the HEX mesh.}
  \label{fig:mesh_selection}
\end{figure}

The meshes are evaluated based on a trade-off between computational efficiency and the two discretization error (\cref{fig:mesh_selection}(a)) and mass conservation error (\cref{fig:mesh_selection}(b)) criterias. \textbf{The HEX mesh at refinement 1 is therefore adopted for the production runs}: with 24k degrees of freedom it reaches 0.30\% on the OV flux and 0.26\% on mass conservation, which the TET mesh does not match even at its finest level (0.82\% and 0.43\% with 487k degrees of freedom, 20 times the cost). Its 3.0\% error on the leakage flux is larger, and the TET mesh does end up below it at its finest level, but this is where the parallelepipedic channel matters: the geometric bias it introduces on that same quantity is 4.7\%, so refining the HEX mesh further would only chase a discretization error that is already smaller than the geometry it is computed on.

The parallelepipedic approximation of the arched channel was replicated on the TET mesh to identify the origin of discrepancies of TET and HEX meshes. The results for the bottom cell are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item TET, original geometry: $\phi_\mathrm{leak}=4.66 \cdot10^9 \ \mathrm{T/s}$
    \item TET, approximated channel: $\phi_\mathrm{leak}=4.41 \cdot10^9 \ \mathrm{T/s}$
    \item HEX, approximated channel: $\phi_\mathrm{leak}=4.40 \cdot10^9 \ \mathrm{T/s}$
\end{itemize}
It shows that a $-5.4\%$ underestimation of the leakage flux can be attributed to the parallelepipedic channel approximation. The geometric difference being removed, HEX and TET meshes agree within 0.5\% of the leakage flux value, which can be attributed to discretization error. This cross-validates the trustworthiness of the model predictions.

\paragraph{Limitations}

The point is not to have a perfect simulation but to know where it stops being trustworthy. Three limitations should be kept in mind when reading the results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mass conservation is the accuracy floor of every accountancy number}. The surface fluxes are recovered from the gradient of a linear field, which does not conserve mass exactly: at the selected mesh, what enters through the salt face and what leaves through the two others differ by 0.26\% in the convergence study, and by up to 1.9\% at the wall loadings actually reached during a run (\cref{fig:permeation_vs_sparging}(b)). This is because the transient runs add a second contribution on top of this one: the time integration is only first order, so the finite time step introduces its own imbalance, which the steady-state convergence study cannot see. This is why the bars of \cref{fig:partition} can slightly exceed 100\% of the bred inventory, and it is the reason no result below the percent level should be read as significant.
    \item \textbf{The parallelepipedic channel biases the leakage flux by 4.7\%}, as quantified above. It is a geometric bias, not a discretization error, so refining does not remove it.
    \item \textbf{The environment-side boundary is a perfect sink}. Any oxide or surface resistance on the outer wall would reduce the leaked fraction, so the leakage numbers reported here are upper bounds.
    \item \textbf{One cell stands in for the 3 lateral faces}. Using the inner cell for the side and outer faces as well hides a spread of the contact ratio of $-4.7\%$ to $+6.9\%$ in relative terms. Since the leaked fraction is assumed to be dictated by the contact area, this propagates directly to the reconstructed leakage.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Confidence in the reported numbers}
Combining the above gives a simple and deliberately conservative error budget on the fractions reported in \cref{sec:perm_results}. Every quantity that crosses a surface of the model carries the mass conservation error, i.e. up to 2\% in relative terms. The leaked fraction carries in addition the $\pm7\%$ of the contact ratio spread, and is subject to 2 biases of opposite sign that are not corrected for: the parallelepipedic channel makes it 5\% too low, the perfect sink on the outer surface makes it too high. We therefore quote \textbf{the collected and retained fractions to $\pm2\%$ and the leaked fraction to $\pm10\%$, in relative terms}. On the headline result of this study, this means $12.0\%$ of the bred tritium leaking becomes $12\pm1\%$ of it.

These are numerical uncertainties only. They say nothing about the uncertainty on the model inputs, which is dominated by the salt-metal interface barrier and is covered explicitly by the PRF sweep, and by $k_e\,c_\mathrm{H_2O}$ for the moisture case.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:perm_results}

\subsection{The salt side sets how much permeates}
\label{sec:perm_salt_side}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/permeation_vs_sparging.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Cumulative tritium removed from the salt, one stacked area per permeation reduction factor, the permeated share at the end of the run printed on the right of each panel. (b) Relative difference of the cumulative permeation of the \ce{H2} and \ce{H2O} cases against the He only case.}
  \label{fig:permeation_vs_sparging}
\end{figure}

The salt-side resistance dominates the two others by several orders of magnitude, and this has a consequence that simplifies the whole analysis: \textbf{the metal never gets close to equilibrium with the salt}. Over a 4-day run the tritium concentration at the salt-facing wall stays between $2.7\cdot10^{11}$ and $1.5\cdot10^{15} \ \mathrm{T/m^3}$, i.e. 5 to 6 orders of magnitude below the $4.0\cdot10^{20} \ \mathrm{T/m^3}$ it would reach at equilibrium. The back-pressure term of \cref{eq:salt_BC} therefore never exceeds $10^{-7}$ of the driving force, and the salt BC reduces in practice to $J_\mathrm{salt}\simeq k_\mathrm{eff}\,c_\mathrm{salt}$.

It follows that \textbf{how much tritium permeates is set by $k_\mathrm{eff}$ alone}. \Cref{fig:permeation_vs_sparging}(a) shows the cumulative tritium leaving the salt for each PRF: $95\pm2\%$, $64\pm1\%$, $15.1\pm0.3\%$ and $1.74\pm0.04\%$ of the bred inventory for $\mathrm{PRF}=1$, 10, 100 and 1000 respectively. The salt-side flux is agnostic of what happens at the OV surface, since it does not depend on the metal surface concentration: the 3 sweep gas compositions give cumulative permeations that differ by less than 0.8\% from each other (\cref{fig:permeation_vs_sparging}(b)), which is of the same order as the mass conservation error of the model itself, and below it at the 2 lowest PRF. A direct consequence is that \textbf{the fraction of tritium recovered by sparging depends on the PRF only}, and not on how the OV gas is composed.

\begin{keyresult}
    \centering
    Permeation is limited by transport on the salt side \\
    $\Rightarrow$ the wall stays 5-6 decades below equilibrium with the salt, and $J_\mathrm{salt}\simeq k_\mathrm{eff}\,c_\mathrm{salt}$ \\
    $\Rightarrow$ how much permeates is set by $k_\mathrm{eff}$, not by the sweep gas
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{The sweep gas sets where it goes}
\label{sec:perm_sweep}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/leakage_ratio_vs_metal_concentration.pdf}
  \caption{Leakage ratio of steady state simulations, as a function of the tritium concentration on the metal side of the salt interface, for the 3 cases of OV surface boundary condition and the 2 extreme cell types. Datapoints are shown as dots. The strip under each panel gives the wall loading reached during a 4-day run for each permeation reduction factor.}
  \label{fig:leakage_vs_loading}
\end{figure}

What the salt side does not decide is where the permeated tritium goes once it is inside the metal. That split is set at the OV surface, and it is the quantity that matters for accountancy. The OV BC being non-linear, it introduces a dependency of the leakage ratio on the wall loading, shown in \cref{fig:leakage_vs_loading}.

In the He only and He + \ce{H2O} cases, the OV flux is \textit{surface limited}. In the case of recombination with \ce{H}, permeation is \textit{diffusion limited}, changing the flux BC to a perfect sink ($c_\mathrm{T}=0$) confirms this. This is valid for the entire range of considered wall loadings, hiding the non-linearity of the OV boundary condition and making the leakage ratio \textbf{loading independent}. This sets a lower bound on the achievable leakage ratio for each geometry.

(Note on terminology: by \textit{surface limited}, it is meant that the bottleneck for tritium permeation is chemical reactions happening \textbf{at the surface}. For example, tritium recombination ($k_r\,c_\mathrm{T}^2$ or $k_r\,c_\mathrm{T}\,c_\mathrm{H}$) or isotopic exchange ($k_e\,c_\mathrm{H_2O}\,c_T$). Conversely, if any of these mechanisms is very fast (e.g. if $k_r\,c_\mathrm{H}$ is large), all the tritium that reaches the surface can be considered immediately transferred to the gas, tritium concentration at the surface is then null. Permeation is no longer limited by reactions happening at the surface but rather by transport \textbf{to the surface}: it is \textit{diffusion limited}.)

\paragraph{The recombination sink is negligible where the wall operates}
Because the wall stays so far from equilibrium (\cref{sec:perm_salt_side}), the quadratic recombination term is evaluated at a concentration that is 5 decades lower than what the equilibrium assumption would suggest. Expressed as a velocity to be compared against the exchange terms, $k_r\,c_\mathrm{T}$ ranges from $1\cdot10^{-16}$ to $6\cdot10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ over a run, against $4.6\cdot10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for the moisture channel and $3.5\cdot10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for the \ce{H2} one. \textbf{Recombination alone is 4 to 7 orders of magnitude too weak to compete with the leakage pathway}: the OV surface is barely a sink at all, and essentially all the permeated tritium ends up leaking to the environment. Adding a reactive partner to the sweep gas is what turns the OV surface into a collector:
\begin{itemize}
    \item He only: leakage ratio of 100\% on both cell types;
    \item He + \ce{H2O}: 35\% (side cell) and 45\% (bottom cell);
    \item He + 3.5\% \ce{H2}: 0.25\% (side cell) and 0.34\% (bottom cell).
\end{itemize}
The 3 curves only start to bend above $10^{17}\ \mathrm{T/m^3}$, which is 2 to 4 decades above anything the wall reaches. \textbf{In the operating range, the leakage ratio is therefore a constant of the geometry and of the sweep gas composition}, and does not depend on how loaded the salt is.

The effect of the geometry is visible in the 2 cases where the OV surface is a sink: the bottom cell leaks 1.3 to 1.4 times more than the side cell, in line with its 1.5 times larger contact ratio (\cref{tab:unit_cell}), as expected from the assumption that leakage is dictated by the spacer contact area. With He alone the comparison is meaningless, since both geometries saturate at 100\% leakage.

\begin{keyresult}
    \centering
    The leakage ratio is set by the OV surface chemistry, and is loading independent in the operating range \\
    He only $\Rightarrow$ 100\% leaks \quad $|$ \quad He + \ce{H2O} $\Rightarrow$ 35-45\% \quad $|$ \quad He + 3.5\% \ce{H2} $\Rightarrow$ 0.3\% \\
    $\Rightarrow$ a reducing sweep gas is what makes the double wall a collector rather than a conduit
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Most of the tritium never leaves the wall}
\label{sec:perm_retention}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/wall_retention.pdf}
  \caption{Vessel-scale tritium inventory held in the structural metal, as a fraction of the bred inventory, for the loosest (left) and the tightest (right) salt-metal interface barrier considered.}
  \label{fig:wall_retention}
\end{figure}

The leakage ratio of \cref{sec:perm_sweep} describes the \textit{branching between OV and leakage} among the tritium that comes out of the metal, but it says nothing about \textit{how much tritium does come out}. It turns out that without \ce{H2} most of it does not: the wall behaves as a reservoir.

\Cref{fig:wall_retention} shows the vessel-scale wall inventory over a run. All 3 compositions load the wall to about the same peak, which is expected since loading is set by the salt side, and they then diverge completely because unloading is set by the OV chemistry. At the end of a 4-day run with $\mathrm{PRF}=1$, \textbf{$84\pm2\%$ of all the tritium bred in the experiment is still inside the structural metal} with He only, and $52\pm1\%$ with He + \ce{H2O}, against essentially nothing with He + 3.5\% \ce{H2}, which empties the wall by 6 orders of magnitude over the same time. The same picture holds at $\mathrm{PRF}=1000$, only 50 times lower in amplitude. Note also that the loading timescale is set by the PRF, the peak being reached after a few hours at $\mathrm{PRF}=1$ but only after 30 to 50 hours at $\mathrm{PRF}=1000$, whereas the unloading timescale is set by the sweep gas alone.

This is important for the robust accountancy goal: tritium sitting in the metal at the end of a run will come out delayed, on a timescale much longer than the run itself. This is accounted for in the BABY experiments by running \ce{H2} in the sweep gas at the end of every run, to collect all the tritium retained in the wall. 


\begin{keyresult}
    \centering
    Without \ce{H2} in the sweep gas, the wall is a reservoir: $84\pm2\%$ (He only) and $52\pm1\%$ (He + \ce{H2O}) \\
    of the bred tritium is still in the metal after 4 days, against $\sim0\%$ with 3.5\% \ce{H2} \\
    $\Rightarrow$ the accountancy does not close on the gas streams alone
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:perm_summary}

\subsubsection{Permeation against sparging}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.1\linewidth]{fig/final_partition.pdf}
  \caption{Fate of the bred tritium at the end of a 4-day run, for each permeation reduction factor and each sweep gas composition, as a fraction of the bred inventory. The leaked percentage is printed above its own band. What remains in the salt is below 0.04\% everywhere and is not shown. Bars can exceed 100\% by the mass conservation error of the model.}
  \label{fig:partition}
\end{figure}

Putting the 3 previous results together gives the answer to \textit{where does the bred tritium end up ?}. \Cref{fig:partition} summarizes the 12 simulated cases, and the corresponding time series are reported in \cref{fig:cumulative}.

\paragraph{Permeation is likely to be significant}
The competition between the 2 removal pathways is entirely decided by the interface barrier. At $\mathrm{PRF}=1$, sparging only recovers 5\% of the bred tritium and permeation takes the remaining 95\%; at $\mathrm{PRF}=1000$ the situation is reversed, sparging recovers 98\%. \textbf{The crossover sits around $\mathrm{PRF}\approx100$}. Since the interface barrier is precisely the least constrained parameter of this model, both situations have to be considered plausible, and the common assumption that permeation is a negligible loss channel compared to sparging \textbf{cannot be made} for LIBRA Pi.

\paragraph{The sweep gas composition decides the accountancy}
For a given PRF, the amount extracted by sparging is the same in the 3 compositions, as established in \cref{sec:perm_salt_side}. What changes is what happens to the permeated fraction, and the difference is significant. At $\mathrm{PRF}=1$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{He only}: $1.22\pm0.02\%$ collected in the OV gas, \textbf{$12\pm1\%$ leaked to the environment}, $84\pm2\%$ still in the wall;
    \item \textbf{He + \ce{H2O}}: $34.0\pm0.7\%$ collected, $10\pm1\%$ leaked, $52\pm1\%$ still in the wall;
    \item \textbf{He + 3.5\% \ce{H2}}: 96.7\% collected, 0.39\% leaked, nothing left in the wall.
\end{itemize}
Adding \ce{H2} to the sweep gas divides the leaked fraction by $\sim30$ and turns a vessel that loses tritium into one that accounts for it. \Cref{fig:partition} shows this holds at every PRF.

\begin{keyresult}
    \centering
    $\mathrm{PRF}\lesssim100 \Rightarrow$ permeation dominates over sparging, and cannot be neglected \\
    He only $\Rightarrow$ up to $12\pm1\%$ of the bred tritium leaks to the environment \\
    He + 3.5\% \ce{H2} in the OV gas $\Rightarrow$ $0.39\pm0.04\%$ leaks and $97\pm2\%$ is collected \\
    $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{a reducing OV sweep gas is required to meet the robust accountancy goal}
\end{keyresult}

\subsubsection{An intuitive representation}
All four results above are statements about which of 5 tritium transport resistances dominates, summarized by \cref{fig:resistances}: looking at the 0D coupling equation (\cref{eq:salt_balance}), extraction by sparging can be represented as a resistance $R_0$ proportional to the sparging extraction time $\tau$ (the longer, the more resistance it opposes to tritium transport); $R_1$, the salt-to-wall resistance, is orders of magnitude larger than the three downstream, which is why the amount that permeates is set by $k_\mathrm{eff}$ alone and ignores the sweep gas composition entirely (\cref{sec:perm_salt_side}).  Tritium leaves the salt either through the bubbles or through the wall, $R_1$ and $R_0$ therefore compete in what portion of the bred tritium they extracts respectively. The wall itself is a reservoir, with a comparatively low diffuse resistance $R_2$. What the sweep gas does control is the partition between $R_3$ and $R_4$, i.e. the split between the tritium that is collected and the tritium that is lost (\cref{sec:perm_sweep}) among what is stored in the wall. When $R_3$ is large (He only case), the wall can't evacuate its tritium, it accumulates (\cref{sec:perm_retention}) with a portion of it slowly leaking to the environment through $R_4$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{diag_resistances}}
  \caption{The permeation study as a resistance network. $R_1$ dominates, so it alone sets how much tritium permeates; the wall has a comparatively small diffusive resistance $R_2$, parallel to a capacitor that stores tritium; $R_3$ variable resistance is set by the sweep gas chemistry.}
  \label{fig:resistances}
\end{figure}

\chapter{Conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

% In the conclusion you repeat the main result and finalize the discussion of
% your project. Mention the core results and why as well as how your system
% advances the status quo.

% summary of what was done
This work set out to answer whether gas sparging can extract tritium from the LIBRA Pi molten salt breeder fast enough to be useful, and more generally what governs the kinetics of a sparging system. It did so by building a one-dimensional advection-reaction-dispersion model of the two phases, deriving a closed-form solution from it, mapping where that closed form can be trusted, and finally applying both to LIBRA Pi alongside a three-dimensional model of tritium permeation through the vessel.

% the main results of the design study
The central result of the modelling is that the kinetics of a sparging system are governed by a single dimensionless number: the \textbf{saturation number} $\langle\Pi\rangle$, which compares the residence time of a bubble to the time it needs to reach equilibrium with the surrounding liquid. It separates two regimes with opposite operation rules: below $\langle\Pi\rangle=0.1$ the bubbles stay far from equilibrium and the extraction is limited by the liquid side mass transfer, improved by increasing temperature; above $\langle\Pi\rangle=5$ the bubbles leave saturated and the extraction is throttled by the gas throughput, raising temperature is counterproductive and the gas flow rate becomes the only useful lever. The closed form $\tau_{ana}=\tau_\mathrm{SPP}\,\langle\Pi\rangle/(1-e^{-\langle\Pi\rangle})$ covers both regimes and the transition between them.

% analytical and numerical models
Mapping the design space showed when that closed form is accurate. Across 300 samples spanning four decades of each governing group, the analytical extraction time reproduces the numerical one to within $\pm5\%$ whenever the system is in the validity regions established by the design map (\cref{tab:design_map}). The criterion is easy to apply: a designer evaluates three dimensionless groups (\Gmix, \Gp, \PI) and knows immediately whether a formula suffices or a numerical solution is required. The same study identified the \textbf{head space pressure as the strongest lever available at fixed gas flow and temperature}: operating at $\Gp>0.1$ by lowering the pressure above the salt improves the extraction time up to a factor 8, as long as bubble coalescence stays negligible.

It should be kept in mind however that the design study covers the whole design space of the ARD model only, that is to say \textbf{to the extent of what the ARD equations are able to model}. This model is itself built on a set of assumptions that restrict the scope of the physics it is able to describe, starting with the approximation that the problem can be reduced to 1D. Some mechanisms might be at play in some sparging systems that go beyond the scope of the ARD model, then requiring more advanced but also more computationally expensive simulations. 

In cases the ARD model is considered suited for the sparging problem to be described, the designer can chose which method (numerical or analytic) to implement, basing the decision on the design map of \cref{tab:design_map}. It can then be coupled to a tritium transport simulation or integrated into a fuel cycle model. Fuel cycle models describe a plant with lumped compartments, they take the performance of a tritium extraction system as an input: in most cases the analytical model holds and coupling boils down to a \textbf{0D mass balance with the analytical extraction time as a lumped surrogate for sparging}, evaluated without running a transport simulation at all. When the analytical model can't describe sparging dynamics accurately, the computationally cheap numerical ARD model becomes the right object to hand to a system-level model. At the other end of the range, three-dimensional CFD simulations describe bubble dynamics and especially the gas-liquid interfaces more accurately, but cannot be swept over an entire design space nor integrated into fuel cycle models for computational cost reasons. This is the gap occupied by the present model.

% sparging in LIBRA Pi
Applied to LIBRA Pi, the model predicts a tritium removal time of \textbf{2.3 to 5.3 days} at the nominal operating point, and between 16 hours and 36 days over the whole attainable operating window. \textbf{The width of that interval represents epistemic uncertainty on the salt, not variability on the operating conditions}: the tritium transport properties of ClLiF have never been measured, and the two bounding scenarios simply carry the most optimistic and the most pessimistic correlations available for related fluoride salts. The sensitivity analysis showed that among the parameters the experiment does control, temperature and gas flow rate carry most of the variance and therefore \textbf{most of the operational uncertainty}, while the head space pressure and the nozzle diameter carry almost none. The end-result of this study is an operating map (\cref{fig:abaque}), which can be used to estimate the extraction time for a given operating point along with the uncertainty associated originated by both the unknown salt properties and the uncertain operating conditions. Using this simple abaque, it was graphically established that \textbf{the salt properties uncertainty dominates the experimental uncertainty}. 

% permeation in LIBRA Pi
Coupling the extraction time to a model of tritium permeation through the double-walled vessel produced the result with the most direct consequence for the experiment. Permeation is limited by transport on the salt side, so the metal never approaches equilibrium with the salt, and how much tritium permeates is set by the salt-metal interface barrier alone. That barrier is the least constrained parameter of the whole study, and over the plausible range it spans, permeation moves from taking $95\%$ of the bred tritium to taking $2\%$: the assumption that permeation is negligible against sparging cannot be made for LIBRA Pi. What the sweep gas between the two walls decides is not how much permeates but where it goes. With helium alone the outer surface is barely a sink at all, up to $12\pm1\%$ of the bred tritium leaks to the experimental room and $84\pm2\%$ remains in the metal at the end of a run; adding $3.5\%$ of \ce{H2} divides the leaked fraction by $\sim30$ and empties the wall. \textbf{A reducing sweep gas is therefore needed to achieve precise accountancy of the bred tritium}.

The fact that \ce{H2} performs better than \ce{H2O} in reacting with adsorbed tritium is a surprising result: it is commonly accepted in the tritium community that \ce{H2O} is a better tritium getter. When modeling this, it all depends on the value used for the isotopic exchange term $k_e\,c_\mathrm{H_2O}$, which here was obtained from an unpublished fit performed by members of the LIBRA group on previous BABY 1L experimental results. Other mechanisms than mere isotopic exchange with moisture might have been at play when doing the fit, biasing the result of the present simulation, which should therefore be interpreted with great caution. The only conclusion that can be drawn in the He+\ce{H2O} case is that moisture significantly helps desorbing tritium (an already known result) and that in doing so \textbf{it does mitigate tritium leakage}. Whether or not \ce{H2} or \ce{H2O} perform better at that is still to be proven experimentally.   


\section{Outlook}

% - can be used to optimize design of sparging system: find right H (spare useless height where the bubble is already saturated)

\paragraph{Model validation}

The most important limitation of this work is that the model is verified but not validated. Its numerical solution was checked against an analytical one derived independently, and its discretisation error quantified, but no prediction has been compared to an experiment. The measurements that would allow a first comparison exist: the stripping record of the MSRE reported by \textcite{briggs_calculation_1970}. LIBRA Pi itself will provide the direct test, and the operating map of \cref{fig:abaque} is the prediction to check it against. For as long as the model is not validated, uncertainty on the tritium transport properties of molten salts remains the limiting factor on any prediction.

\paragraph{A novel method for measuring tritium transport properties}

The present modeling work opens the possibility to \textbf{use experimental sparging data of LIBRA Pi to infer tritium transport properties of ClLiF}: if the system is operated at \PI<0.1, diffusivity of tritium in ClLiF can be obtained from the extraction time and the Sherwood number correlation, considering that the model predicts $\tau\propto \frac{\varepsilon_l}{a\,h_l}\propto \frac{\varepsilon_l\,d_b}{a\,\mathrm{Sh}\,D_l}$. This method doesn't have the drawback of having to first permeate tritium through structural metal. It however does require the properties of the bubbles $a$ and $h_l$ to be known or measured, this is typically something that CFD sparging simulations can resolve.    

\paragraph{Improving the models}

Several assumptions (see \cref{sec:sparging_assumptions}) could be relaxed without changing the structure of the model. Tritium is treated as a single chemical species, which the BABY results support but which a change of redox conditions would invalidate; carrying \ce{TF} alongside \ce{T2} would require only a second transport equation and an equilibrium between them. The same applies to isotopic exchange in the gas phase, which the BABY campaigns suggest is the dominant release channel and which the present model does not represent. 

The closure relations themselves deserve more attention: different combinations of hydrodynamic correlations should be explored to challenge the robustness of the set of closure relations chosen in this work, the automatic resolution stage making it easy to swap one correlation for another; also the choice of which correlation to use could be made automatically from the properties of the flow rather than left to the user.

Regarding the permeation model, a key weakness is that no temporal convergence study was performed. This is the reason attributed to the imperfect mass conservation evidenced in the results of the transient simulation, exceeding unity by $\sim 2\%$ in the worst cases. In the future, this would deserve a dedicated mesh temporal convergence study to be able to quantify the magnitude of the error the finite time step brings, as was done for the sparging model. 

\paragraph{Recommendations for LIBRA Pi operation}

Three practical recommendations for the operation of the experiment arise from the analysis done in this work: (1) the temperature must be measured precisely at several points, since it is the parameter the extraction time is most sensitive to and the least controllable model input; (2) potential validation campaign for the sparging system should be run at high temperature and vary the gas flow rate, because the isolines of \cref{fig:abaque} become parallel to the temperature axis there, so an error on the temperature propagates least; (3) the sweep gas circulating between the two vessel walls should contain a few percent of \ce{H2}, as already achieved in BABY 1L and now crucial in LIBRA Pi, since it is what turns the double wall from a leakage highway into a tritium collector.

\label{app:tau_full}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This appendix repeats the derivation of \cref{sec:analytical_solution} without the uniform hydrodynamics assumption (\cref{as:uniform_hydrodynamic}) used there to reach the compact form of \cref{eq:tau_ana}. The starting point is the same steady gas equation (\cref{eq:PT_steady}), but $a$, $h_l$ and $u_g$ now keep their dependence on $z$.

Introducing the tritium molar flux per unit area $G\equiv\frac{u_g\,\PT}{R\,T}$, and recognizing that $\frac{a\,h_l\,K_s\,R\,T}{u_g}=\frac{d\Pi^\star}{dz}$ (\cref{eq:Pi_cumul}), \cref{eq:PT_steady} takes the form of a first order linear ODE:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dG}{dz}+\frac{d\Pi^\star}{dz}\,G=a\,h_l\,\langle\CT\rangle^l
\end{equation}
which is integrated with the factor $e^{\Pi^\star(z)}$. Gas dispersion being neglected, \cref{eq:BC1} imposes a tritium free sparging gas at the inlet, $G(0)=0$, and it comes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PT_profile_gen}
    \PT(z)=\frac{R\,T\,\langle\CT\rangle^l}{u_g(z)}\int_0^z a(s)\,h_l(s)\,e^{-\left(\Pi^\star(z)-\Pi^\star(s)\right)}\,ds
\end{equation}
The exponential weight of \cref{eq:PT_profile_gen} expresses that the tritium transferred to a bubble at height $s$ is progressively "forgotten" as the bubble keeps rising towards equilibrium with the liquid. Should the hydrodynamic parameters be uniform, $a$, $h_l$ and $u_g$ come out of the integral and \cref{eq:PT_profile_gen} reduces to the single bubble result of \cref{eq:PT_profile}.

\Cref{eq:PT_profile_gen} can be evaluated at the gas outlet and plugged into the expression of the outgoing tritium flux (\cref{eq:dotNT}). The outlet velocity $u_g(H)$ cancels out, leaving the interfacial transfer integrated over the column height, damped by the saturation of the bubbles:
\begin{equation}
    \dotNT=-A\,u_g\frac{\PT}{R\,T}\bigg\rvert_\mathrm{outlet}=-A\,\langle\CT\rangle^l\int_0^H a(z)\,h_l(z)\,e^{-\left(\langle\Pi\rangle-\Pi^\star(z)\right)}dz
\end{equation}
Given the assumed negligible bubble residence time (\cref{as:short_res_time}), we can consider that every tritium that goes into the gas phase is immediately evacuated out of the system, therefore $\NTg\ll\NTl \Rightarrow \NTl\approx\NT$. Simplifying $\langle \CT\rangle^l=\frac{\NTl}{V_l}=\frac{\NTl}{\langle \varepsilon_l\rangle V_t}$ with $V_t=A\,H$ yields:
\begin{equation}
    \dotNT=-\frac{\int_0^H a(z)\,h_l(z)\,e^{-\left(\langle\Pi\rangle-\Pi^\star(z)\right)}dz}{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle\,H}\,\NT
\end{equation}

Solving yields an exponential decay, as obtained previously under the small partial pressure approximation (\cref{sec:extraction_kinetics}), here generalized for any partial pressure regime:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tau_full}
    \boxed{\tau =\frac{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle\,H}{\int_0^H a(z)\,h_l(z)\,e^{-\left(\langle\Pi\rangle-\Pi^\star(z)\right)}dz}}
\end{equation}

This exact general form behaves as expected in both limit regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\langle\Pi\rangle\ll1$ (SPP regime): the exponential weight is unity over the whole column, every height contributes equally to the transfer and $\tau\rightarrow\frac{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle}{\langle a\,h_l \rangle}=\tau_\mathrm{SPP}$, consistently with \cref{eq:tau_SPP} and without requiring any further assumption;
    \item $\langle\Pi\rangle\gg1$ (PPL regime): the weight collapses onto the top of the column, the bubbles leave saturated at $\PT(H)=\frac{\langle\CT\rangle^l}{K_s}$ and $\tau\rightarrow\frac{\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle\,H\,R\,T\,K_s}{u_g(H)}$, i.e. the extraction is throttled by the gas throughput at the outlet rather than by the interfacial mass transfer.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Systematic bias of the backward Euler scheme on the extraction time}
\label{app:dt_bias}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This appendix proves the result used in \cref{sec:numerical_error}: the time discretisation shifts the fitted extraction time by $\frac{\Delta t}{2}$, always in the same direction.

The extraction time is not read directly from the simulated inventory but obtained by fitting an exponential decay to $\NT(t)$ over the window $[0,2\tau]$, and that fitted value carries a systematic error inherited from the time discretisation. The ARD equations being linear, once the initial transient has died out the space-discretised inventory relaxes along its slowest eigenmode $\dotNT=-\NT/\tau$; advancing it with the backward Euler scheme of the solver gives the geometric sequence $\NT^{k}=\NT^{0}(1+\Delta t/\tau)^{-k}$, so the discrete solution is still \emph{exactly} exponential, only with a modified time constant $\tau_\Delta$ such that $1/\tau_\Delta=\ln(1+x)/\Delta t$, writing $x\equiv\Delta t/\tau$. Expanding for $x\ll1$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tau_delta_expansion}
    \frac{\tau_\Delta}{\tau}=\frac{x}{\ln(1+x)}=1+\frac{x}{2}-\frac{x^{2}}{12}+O(x^{3})
    \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
    \tau_\Delta \simeq \tau+\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\end{equation}
The discretisation error on $\tau$ is therefore systematic rather than statistical: the fit itself contributes no scatter, since the numerical inventory is an exact exponential and the fit returns $\tau_\Delta$ rather than an estimate of it, and its sign is fixed because $x/\ln(1+x)>1$ for all $x>0$, so backward Euler always over-estimates the extraction time, by
\begin{equation*}
    e_\Delta \equiv \frac{\tau_\Delta-\tau}{\tau}\simeq\frac{\Delta t}{2\,\tau}
\end{equation*}
which is \cref{eq:e_delta}.
