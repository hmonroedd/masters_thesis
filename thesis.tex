\section{The tritium isotope}
% what is tritium (main characteristics)
Tritium is a radioactive isotope of hydrogen, its nucleus is made of 1 proton and 2 neutrons and is therefore denoted \ce{^3H} or \ce{T}. It disintegrates into \ce{^3He} through beta decay with a half life of 12.3 years. 
% where do we find it
Because of its short half life and its absence of any decay chain, tritium can't be naturally found in concentrated quantities unlike other radioactive isotopes (e.g. uranium-238, radon-222). It is unintentionally produced: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item naturally, from highly energetic cosmic neutrons interacting with atmospheric nitrogen (\ce{^14_7N(n,T)^12_6C} reaction, contribution estimated at 0.2 kg/year)
    \item as a product of the fission reactions in all fission reactors, with a low yield ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$ T/fission event, depending on the fissile nuclide and the neutron energy) \needcite{IAEA database} %https://www-nds.iaea.org/relnsd/vcharthtml/VChartHTML.html
\end{itemize}

% do we want tritium ?
Even though beta radiations don't penetrate the skin, tritium can substitute to hydrogen in water to form \ce{HTO}, which if spread in the environment can contaminate the food chain and become a radiological hazard. For that reason, it is an undesirable byproduct in the fission industry. % -> no in fission

Still, tritium finds a use as a fuel in nuclear fusion: because of its favorable cross section for fusion reactions with deuterium (\ce{^2H} or \ce{D}), it was identified as the most efficient fuel for fusion devices \needcite{ITER physical basis report ?}. Historically, nuclear fusion and therefore tritium have been serving military purposes, in nuclear weapons. But just like nuclear fission found a peaceful use in nuclear reactors for energy production, controlled nuclear fusion offers a promising path to carbon-free energy. Compared to its fission counterpart, a fusion power plant (FPP) would present the advantage of lower and shorter radiotoxicity of its byproducts, increased safety and abundance of fuel. Controlled fusion has therefore been the subject of intense research for decades, and is now entering a fast growing phase: in 2026, the Fusion Industry Association counted 56 private fusion companies for a total funding of \$14.2bn worldwide, which is \$4.5bn more than in 2025\cite{noauthor_2026_2026}. Among these companies, 40 of them envision deuterium-tritium (DT) as fusion fuel. Deuterium is present in small proportions in seawater (\needcite{how many ppm ?}) and its extraction is a well known process as evidenced by the fleet of 44 Pressurized Heavy Water Reactors (PHWR) operating worldwide. Tritium fuel supply on the other hand is a much bigger concern due to itsp scarcity, stressing the need for industrial tritium production. % -> yes in fusion

\section{Tritium production}
\subsection{Main pathways}
% how can we create tritium 
A typical FPP would require important amounts of tritium, estimated at $\sim$55 kg/year per GW of fusion power \cite{abdou_physics_2020}. The current world inventory of $\sim$20 kg of tritium is largely insufficient and halves every 12 years due to radioactive decay if not renewed. It is then necessary to intentionally produce tritium via nuclear transmutation:
\begin{align}
%include reaction with B10 ? -> in PWR 
    \ce{^2H + n &-> T + \gamma} \label{eq:D2_capture}\\
    \ce{^6Li + n &-> T + \alpha} + 4.8~\mathrm{MeV} \label{eq:Li6_capture}\\
    \ce{^7Li + n &-> T + \alpha} + \mathrm{n} - 2.5~\mathrm{MeV} \label{eq:Li7_capture}
\end{align}

As of now, the existing commercially available tritium is produced as a byproduct of heavy water moderated reactors through neutron capture (\cref{eq:D2_capture}), specifically in CANada Deuterium Uranium,(CANDU) reactors (estimated at 130 g of tritium/reactor/year) \needcite{https://www.neimagazine.com/analysis/answering-the-big-tritium-question/?cf-view}. But the small cross section of this reaction (0.5 millibarns for thermal neutrons) makes it unsuitable for large scale production. \Cref{eq:Li6_capture} on the other hand  has a 1000~barns thermal neutron cross section \needcite{JANIS database (https://www.oecd-nea.org/janisweb/book/neutrons/H2/MT102/renderer/3458)}, it is used for military tritium production and is also the privileged path to tritium breeding for controlled fusion. Industrial tritium production would therefore rely on thermal neutron capture in a lithium-6 rich material, the cross section for \cref{eq:Li7_capture} being marginal (300 millibarns, for fast neutrons).

Aforementioned transmutation reactions all need neutrons as a reactant. Neutrons can be found in fission reactors, but producing tritium there poses the problem of introducing large amounts of tritium in an environment where it is usually unwanted because of its handling difficulties and the associated radiological concern, and is anyway constrained to relatively low tritium amounts to leave enough neutrons for fission to self-sustain ($\sim$1 kg/reactor/year using Tritium Producing Burnable Absorbers Rods)\needcite{NRC?}. For those reasons, tritium production in fission reactors is restricted to military programs.

DT fusion itself produces fast neutrons (\cref{eq:DT_fusion}), it naturally follows that a FPP can act as a neutron source that \textit{breeds} its own fuel. Contrary to external sources such as fission reactors or neutron beams, this approach offers the advantage that the available neutron flux scales with the amount of fuel needed and thus can sustain operation of an arbitrarily large fleet of FPP. This makes breeding the most scalable and economical path to industrial tritium fuel production.

\subsection{The breeding blanket}
% how is breeding engineered ?
In practice, a FPP would produce tritium in a \textit{breeding blanket} that surrounds the fusion core, be it inertially or magnetically confined. For \cref{eq:Li6_capture,eq:Li7_capture} to occur, the breeding blanket is made of a lithium-containing material, chosen among:
\begin{itemize}
    \item solid ceramics: lithium orthosilicate (\ce{Li4SiO4}), lithium metatitanate (\ce{Li2TiO3})
    \item liquid metals: Lithium-Lead eutectic (\ce{Pb_{84}Li_{16}}), melting at 235°C
    \item molten salts: FLiBe (\ce{2LiF-BeF2}), FLiNaK (\ce{LiF-NaF-KF}), melting at $\sim$450°C
\end{itemize}

% material choice: tritium production
From the perspective of tritium production optimization, the choice of breeding material is driven by: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item a high macroscopic neutron capture cross section: higher Li density, \ce{ ^6Li} enrichment is favorable
    \item presence of a neutron moderator: the \ce{^6Li} reaction occurs with thermal neutrons, therefore light elements are favorable for their moderating capability
    \item presence of a neutron multiplier: Pb and Be containing materials readily incorporate a neutron multiplier ((n,2n) reaction)
\end{itemize}

% the other roles of the BB
Beyond the tritium production aspect, a breeding blanket must shield the 14 MeV neutrons produced by the fusion core, i.e. 80\% of the fusion energy (see \cref{eq:DT_fusion}) dissipates in it as heat. In that regard, liquid materials offer the advantage of being both tritium breeding materials and coolant. Molten salts in particular have a heat capacity superior to that of LiPb and don't conduct electricity which makes them favorable for compact and magnetically confined FPP designs, such as the Affordable-Robust-Compact (ARC) tokamak concept\cite{sorbom_arc_2015}.

\section{Challenges of the tritium fuel cycle}
The fusion fuel cycle is the set of processes that supplies tritium to the plasma, divided in two independent closed loops: % "independent": outer loop doesn't have the output of inner loop as input, contrary to what the "inner" and "outer" denominations suggest
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{inner fuel cycle}: recovery of what the plasma does not burn. It is characterized by the fueling efficiency (performance of the fuel injection system, i.e. how much fuel reaches the fusion core) and the tritium burn fraction TBF (performance of the fusion core, i.e. how much tritium fuses among the one that reached the core). The product of these two quantities isn't expected to exceed 5\% \cite{abdou_physics_2020}
    \item \textit{outer fuel cycle}: newly bred tritium to replace what was consumed. It is characterized by the Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR), defined as the ratio of tritium bred per tritium consumed. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Self-sufficiency}
% definition of the scientific and engineering TBR, tritium self-sufficiency of a FPP
% include doubling time considerations, achievable and required TBR ? (Ferry 2023, Abdou 2020)
For the fusion power industry to be able to scale, individual FPPs have to produces at least as much tritium as they consumes, i.e. optimize their outer fuel cycle to achieve a sufficiently high TBR. For explanatory purposes. a distinction can be made between the "scientific" TBR (\cref{eq:TBR_sci}), which only measures tritium being born in the breeding material, and the "engineering" TBR (\cref{eq:TBR_eng}) which measures the tritium that is actually retrieved and can be injected in the fusion core. 
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{TBR_{sci}}&= \frac{\mathrm{T_{bred}}}{\mathrm{T_{burnt}}} \label{eq:TBR_sci} \\
    \mathrm{TBR_{eng}} &= \frac{\mathrm{T_{retrieved}}}{\mathrm{T_{burnt}}} =  \mathrm{TBR_{sci}} \cdot \eta  \label{eq:TBR_eng}\\
    \eta &= \frac{\mathrm{T_{retrieved}}}{\mathrm{T_{bred}}}<1 \label{eq:eta}
\end{align}

Where $\eta$ accounts for losses through the outer fuel cycle. 

$\mathrm{TBR_{sci}}$>1 is necessary, but it is not sufficient. A self sufficient FPP needs to achieve $\mathrm{TBR_{eng}}$>1 by maximizing both its components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{TBR_{sci}}$ is purely neutronics dependent, it is optimized by use of \ce{^6Li} enrichment and by maximizing its thickness as well as the solid angle it covers around the fusion core;
    \item $\eta$ is optimized by reducing permeation losses throughout the fuel cycle (e.g. by means of dedicated structural materials and coatings) and optimizing tritium extraction from the breeder.
\end{itemize}  

\subsection{Tritium handling}
% tritium is very mobile
As isotopes of the same element share same electron structure, tritium chemically behaves like hydrogen (notwithstanding a few nuances in their kinetic properties arising from their mass difference). Tritium forms \ce{T2} gas and can substitutes with hydrogen to form tritiated water \ce{HTO}. It can dissolve as atomic \ce{T} in metallic materials, as a consequence of hydrogen being a small but non-inert element, enabling a 3-step process called \textit{permeation}: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item tritium bonds with the conduction band electrons at the metal surface (dissociation of \ce{T2} into adsorbed \ce{T})
    \item it then fits into interstitial sites of the lattice (high \textit{solubility} in metals) and hop between them over a low energy barrier (high \textit{diffusivity})
    \item  until it reaches the opposite free surface where it becomes \ce{T2} gas again (recombination and desorption)
\end{enumerate}
Permeation is characterized by \textit{permeability} = diffusivity $\times$ solubility, and makes tritium especially hard to contain no matter how leak-tight and defect-free the container is. 

Permeation represents a significant part of outer fuel cycle losses (i.e. degrades $\eta$) and can pose a radiological issue. This is especially the case in liquid breeders that need to operate above a certain melting point, since permeability increases with temperature.

\section{State of the art and existing gaps}

Both inner and outer fuel cycle are the subject of intense research, with the aim not to lose tritium and maximize its production. 

On the outer fuel cycle side, the main focus is to better understand tritium handling and extraction, in other words to be able to predict and maximize $\mathrm{TBR_{eng}}$. In the context of the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), the OFC aspect of ceramic and liquid metal based breeders was investigated in several studies\needcite{ITER references}. 

There is however large uncertainties when it comes to molten salt based breeders, due to the challenges involved in experimenting with them: high temperatures, corrosion, Be toxicity and general tritium handling. This gap is addressed by the Liquid Immersion Blanket:Robust Accountancy (LIBRA) experiment, which main goal is to demonstrate accurate prediction of tritium breeding (the $\mathrm{TBR_{sci}}$ component) and extraction (the $\eta$ component), in a FLiBe salt breeder and DT fusion conditions (14 MeV neutrons)\cite{ferry_libra_2023}. 

Early results from this program are encouraging. The BABY 1L campaign, conducted on litre-scale salt volumes, showed good tritium recovery without any dedicated extraction system: tritium was collected simply by letting it diffuse through the salt to the free surface, or permeate through the container walls \cite{delaporte-mathurin_baby_2026}. At this scale, passive transport is sufficient.

This approach does not survive scaling, as the characteristic time for tritium to diffuse out of the salt grows quadratically with the salt dimensions. The forthcoming LIBRA Pi experiment will operate on a salt volume of $\sim$100\,L, its characteristic diffusion time is $\frac{L^2}{D}=\frac{(30\ \mathrm{cm})^2}{5\cdot10^{-9} \ \mathrm{m^2/s}}\approx200 \ \mathrm{days}$ if no convective transport is considered. Such a timescale is incompatible with the experimental iteration rate the program requires, and an active extraction system therefore becomes necessary.

Gas sparging was identified as a candidate to fill this role. The principle is to bubble an inert gas (typically helium) through the liquid breeder: tritium transfers from the liquid to the rising bubbles across the gas-liquid interface and is carried out of the tank, while the motion of the bubbles simultaneously promotes advective tritium transport within the liquid itself. The process is affordable, mechanically simple, and well documented in the chemical engineering literature \needcite{BCR / sparging chemical engineering references}.

\section{Mathematical framework}

\subsection{Mass transfer}
% expose diffusivity, boundary layer model, then advective transport 
Transport through the liquid-gas interface created by bubbles is what directly allows tritium extraction from the liquid breeder. 
It relies primarily on diffusion, where the density gradient occurring at the interface drives a particle flux $\JT \:\mathrm{[mol/m^2/s]}$ from the tritium-rich liquid to the tritium-poor gas, hereafter the \textit{interfacial flux}. On top of pure diffusion, turbulence at the liquid-gas interface tends to promote mass exchange between phases. 
% It depends on two fundamental properties of the solute-solvant couple (here tritium-molten salt): diffusivity $D_T \:\mathrm{[m^2/s]}$ and solubility $K_s$  ($\mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa]}$ or $\mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa^{0.5}]}$ depending on the solubility law).
\\
Transport of solute gases through liquid-gas interfaces is commonly described by the the Lewis-Whitman two-film model \cite{lewis_principles_1924}. In molten salts, \ce{H_2} has a very low solubility \needcite{H2 low solubility}, so mass transfer through the interface is limited by the liquid film, the interfacial flux is therefore expressed using the liquid side mass transfer coefficient $h_l \:\mathrm{[m/s]}$ which characterized both molecular diffusion and turbulent transport through the liquid film. The interfacial flux is expressed as:

\todo{insert illustration of two fluid model}

\begin{equation*}
    \JT = h_l \,(\CT-c_\mathrm{T_2,i})
\end{equation*}

Where $\CT \: \mathrm{[mol/m^3]}$ is the tritium concentration in the bulk liquid phase, $c_\mathrm{T_2,i}$ is the tritium concentration at the liquid-gas interface.
\par
Concentration at the liquid-gas interface is assumed to be always at equilibrium, dictated by the solubility in the liquid phase: $c_{T_2,i}=c_{T_2,eq}=K_s \,P_{T_2}$, where $P_{T_2} \:\mathrm{[Pa]}$ is the tritium partial pressure in the gas phase, and the solubility of the tritium follows a Henry's law with constant $K_s \: \mathrm{[mol/m^3/Pa]}$ as it is the case for molten salts. It follows:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:JT2}
    \JT = h_l \, (c_{T_2}-K_s\,\PT)
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:JT2}, \JT and \PT are the unknown to be solved in the 1D-ARD model; $K_s$ is a salt property; $h_l$ is a parameter that depends on tritium diffusivity and fluid dynamic characteristics of the salt.

\subsection{Fluid dynamics}
% expose correlations
Advective transport through fluid motion is expected to influence, if not dominate, the tritium concentration and partial pressure profiles in the liquid and gas phases respectively. In a 2D or 3D model, it would require to solve for the velocity field of both phases. Because this work's purpose is to be exploratory, a good pragmatic choice is the simpler one-dimensional axial dispersion model presented by Levenspiel \cite{levenspiel_chemical_1999}, broadly used in chemical reactors modeling \cite{schluter_modeling_1992}. The gas upward velocity is described by the bubble's terminal velocity $u_g \:\mathrm{[m/s]}$, and the liquid and gas mixing effects are described by empirical dispersion coefficients $E_l$ and $E_g$ ($\mathrm{[m^2/s]}$)\textbf{}. The temperature $T$ is assumed constant in time and space. 
\par

The net bubble velocity gives rise to an advective term, while phases mixing translates into a dispersion term. Mixing is expressed by a "dispersion" term that resembles molecular diffusion, as both phenomena have a Fickian behavior which tends to flatten the concentration profiles. They are however inherently different from a physical point of view and shouldn't be mixed up, as transport caused by phases mixing largely dominates molecular diffusion at macroscopic scales. This translates in dispersion coefficients being order of magnitude larger than diffusion coefficients.

\subsubsection{Definitions}

The system to be described is made of a liquid ($l$, the molten salt) and a gas phase ($g$, inert gas bubbles), enclosed in a tank ($t$) that delimits the domain. The concentration field in each phase $\alpha$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    c^\dag_\alpha(\vec r,t)\equiv \left \{ \begin{array}{rcl}
         &  \frac{d n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}(\vec r,t)}{d V_\alpha(\vec r)} , & \vec r\in\alpha\\
         &  0 & \vec r \notin \alpha
    \end{array}\right.
\end{equation}

This corresponds to a phase specific or so-called \textit{intrinsic} quantity of the phase (as defined by \cite{whitaker_method_1999}), in the fact that it represents the accumulation of a species \textit{per unit volume of the phase} ($d V_\alpha$) and not \textit{per unit volume of the porous medium} ($d V_t$). Due to the inherently discontinuous nature of two-fluid systems, such concentrations field is defined and continuous only in its respective phase.

Such definition is physically intuitive, it doesn't hide the discontinuous nature of the system and is therefore conceptually useful to understand the model presented hereafter. However, working with a discontinuous function is inconvenient. We can use the spatial smoothing methodology presented in \cite{gray_derivation_1975} \todo{check consistency with the paper} and define a \textit{Representative Elementary Volume} (REV) denoted $\delta V_t$. It can be seen as a mesoscale volume: large compared to bubbles/pores (microscale, $dV_t$) but small compared to the tank (macroscale, $V_t$). Let us then extend the definition of $c^\dag_\alpha$ to a field $c_\alpha$ that is defined and homogeneous in the whole REV, and introduce the concept of phase fraction of $\alpha$:

% In this two-fluid system, the concentration fields of the liquid and gas phases (respectively $\CT \ \mathrm{[molT_2/m^3]}$ and $\PT \ \mathrm{[Pa]}$) are defined 
\begin{align}
    % \CT(z,t) &\equiv \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},l}(z,t)}{\delta V_l(z)} \label{eq:def_CT} \\
    % \PT(z,t) &\equiv \frac{1}{RT} \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},g}(z,t)}{\delta V_g(z)} \label{eq:def_PT}\\
    c_\alpha (\vec r,t) &\equiv \frac{\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}(\vec r,t)}{\delta V_\alpha(\vec r)} \label{eq:def_c_generic}\\
    \varepsilon_\alpha(\vec r) &\equiv \frac{\delta V_\alpha(\vec r)}{\delta V_t(\vec r)} \label{eq:def_eps_generic}
\end{align}

Where $\delta V_\alpha$ is the volume of phase $\alpha$ contained in $\delta V_t$ (the REV), and $\delta n_{\mathrm{T_2},\alpha}$ is the amount of \ce{T_2} in $\delta V_\alpha$. The field $c_\alpha$ is continuous everywhere in the domain, but because it was artificially extended we can't integrate it as such over the whole domain: this would overestimate the amount of species present in phase $\alpha$, because we would account for a non-zero concentration in regions of the domain where it was actually zero in the discontinuous definition $c^\dag_\alpha$. $\varepsilon_\alpha$ ensure that the total quantity of species $n_\alpha$ is conserved between the continuous and discontinuous definitions: 
\begin{equation}
    n_\alpha\equiv\iiint_{V_t}dn_\alpha= \iiint_{V_\alpha}c^\dag_\alpha\,dV_\alpha=\iiint_{V_t}\varepsilon_\alpha \,c_\alpha\,dV_t
\end{equation}

 \subsubsection{Governing differential equations}
Bringing mass transfer and fluid dynamics together, the equations for tritium transport in a two-phase mixture of liquid and gas can be adapted from the existing literature on transport in a porous media. Except when needed, the space ($z$) and time ($t$) dependences are implicit throughout this work for clarity. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Liquid: 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:CT2}
        \varepsilon_l \frac{\partial \CT}{\partial t} =  \underbrace{\varepsilon_l E_{l} \frac{\partial ^2 \CT}{\partial z^2}}_\text{liquid dispersion} + \underbrace{S_\mathrm{T_2}}_\text{tritium source} - \underbrace{a\,\JT}_{\text{mass transfer}}
    \end{equation}
    \item Gas: 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:PT2}
        \varepsilon_g \frac{1}{RT} \frac{\partial \PT}{\partial t} = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{RT}\frac{\partial (\,u_g\,\PT)}{\partial z}}_\text{gas advection} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_g E_{g} \frac{1}{RT}\frac{\partial ^2 \PT}{\partial z^2}}_\text{gas dispersion} + a\,\JT
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

where $a \:\mathrm{[m^2/m^3]}$ is the specific interfacial area per unit volume, $\varepsilon_g$ and $\varepsilon_l$ are the gas and liquid phase fractions, $u_g \: \mathrm{[m/s]}$ is the \textbf{superficial} gas phase velocity (not to be confused with the true bubbles velocity, previous work on the topic of tritium extraction can be ambiguous). This adaptation of \cite{gray_derivation_1975} intentionally omits molecular diffusion and mesoscale dispersion terms, as these are expected to be largely hidden by the system scale dispersion terms presented here.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\Cref{eq:CT2,eq:PT2} form a system of two partial differential equations coupled through \cref{eq:JT2}, called \textbf{1D Advection-Reaction-Dispersion (ARD)} model. It needs 4 boundary conditions (BCs) to have a unique solution.

\subsubsection{Derivation from mass conservation considerations}

This model is about gas sparging for tritium extraction from the liquid salt where it is bred, the life of a tritium atom that it aims to describe is as follows: (1) tritium gets born in the salt from neutron interaction (abstracted as a known source term $S_\mathrm{T_2}$); (2) transfers from the liquid to the gas phase (through an unknown interfacial flux \JT); (3) gets advected by the bubbles ($u_g$) up to the gas outlet where it is eventually considered as extracted. Note that tritium is constrained to leave the tank \textbf{solely via the gas phase}. It is a deliberate choice of this model not to consider other extraction mechanisms such as permeation from the liquid to the structural material or direct molecular diffusion from the liquid top free-surface to the sweep gas above. 

The tritium path (1),(2) and (3) translate into constraints on the evolution of the tritium inventory $\dot n_\alpha \ \mathrm{[mol/s]}$ in each phase, which will condition the choice of BCs. They are expressed as follows: 
\begin{align}
    \dot n_l &= \stackrel{(1)}{\iiint_V S_\mathrm{T_2} \,dV}\stackrel{(2)}{- \iiint_V a\JT \,dV} \label{eq:n_l_constraint}\\
    \dot n_g &= \stackrel{(3)}{A \,(u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{inlet}} - u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{outlet}})} + \stackrel{(2)}{\iiint_V a\JT \,dV} \label{eq:n_g_constraint}
\end{align}
Where $c_g \equiv\frac{\PT}{RT}$ is the concentration in the gas phase, $A$ is the base area of the tank and $H$ its height. Note $c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{inlet}}$ is kept to stay general, but is eventually null so that (1) is the only way tritium is introduced in the system (i.e. the sparging gas is pure He).

\todo{fix inconsistency with ng notation (used for the molar flow rate of gas itself)} % maybe switch to n_{T_2,gas}

The BCs that enforce this constraint are obtained by integrating \cref{eq:CT2,eq:PT2} over the tank volume:
\begin{align}
    \dot n_l &= \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_V \varepsilon_g \,c_l \,dV =\iiint_V \varepsilon_g \, \frac{\partial c_l}{\partial t}\,dV  \nonumber\\
    &\stackrel{\eqref{eq:CT2}}{=} A\,\bigg(\varepsilon_l\,E_l\,\frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=H} -\varepsilon_l\,E_l\,\frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0} \bigg)- \iiint_V a\JT \,dV  \label{eq:n_l_integrated}
\end{align}
Where $c_l\equiv \CT$ is the \ce{T2} concentration in the liquid phase, used in this derivation for consistency with the gas phase notation $c_g$. Similarly:
\begin{align}
    \dot n_g &\stackrel{\eqref{eq:PT2}}{=}  A \,\bigg(u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0} - u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=H} \,+ \varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=H} -\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0} \bigg) + \iiint_V a\JT \,dV \label{eq:n_g_integrated}
\end{align}

In these expressions, it can be seen that mathematical dispersion terms can contribute to tritium leaving the tank, via a "dispersive current" $\varepsilon_\alpha\,E_\alpha\,\frac{\partial c_\alpha}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0,H}$, analogous to leakage due to molecular diffusion out of a system. Yet, in reality, physical mixing which dispersion terms describe doesn't make the phase being mixed "leak" at the boundaries of the tank. It is especially striking in the case of the liquid phase, which doesn't have a net flow and thus never enters nor leaves the system by any means (as illustrated by the blue dashed arrows in \cref{fig:sparging_tank}). 

Equating \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} and \cref{eq:n_g_integrated} yields the BCs that make compatible the mathematical formulation and the physics to be described: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item in the case of the liquid phase: it forces a zero concentration gradient at the boundaries: 
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial c_l}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=0,H}=0
        \end{equation*}
    
    \item in the case of the gas phase: a concentration gradient is expected to exist due to the non-zero advection term. The gas concentration at the inlet is known, \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} can thus be satisfied by allowing a discontinuity between the gas concentration there ($z=\mathrm{inlet}=0^-$) and the continuous concentration profile in the system. The resulting concentration jump introduces an advective transport contribution that cancels unphysical dispersion-induced leakage at the inlet. This type of 3rd type (Robin) BC is known as a \textit{Danckwerts boundary condition} in the chemical engineering literature: 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0^-}= u_g\,c_g\rvert_{z=0} -\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0}
        \end{equation*}
    
    The gas concentration at the outlet on the other hand is the unknown we typically want the model to predict, thus continuity is required here ($c_g\rvert_{z=H}=c_g\rvert_{z=\mathrm{outlet}}$), and \cref{eq:n_g_constraint} imposes a zero-gradient: 
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial c_g}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
        \end{equation*}
    
    This set of boundary conditions for the gas phase is typically refered to as "closed-closed" in the literature\cite{levenspiel_chemical_1999,mohan_experimental_2010}
\end{itemize} 

\subsubsection{Summary}
\Crefrange{eq:BC1}{eq:BC4} presents the set of 4 boundary conditions previously derived, that enforce the desired tritium extraction physics for the 1D ARD model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the gas inlet, the sparging gas is considered pure He free of any tritium ($c_g\rvert_{z=0^-}=0$), the Danckwert BC becomes:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC1}
        u_g\,\PT\rvert_{z=0} =\varepsilon_g\,E_g\,\frac{\partial \PT}{\partial z}\bigg\rvert_{z=0}
    \end{equation}
    \item At the top of the salt, unlike at the inlet, the gas flow is undisturbed, the tritium concentration stays flat at the transitions from the salt to the free head space:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC2}
        \frac{\partial\PT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
    \end{equation}
    \item Tritium transport due to phase mixing is restrained to the tank volume, and tritium can only escape the system by being advected in the gas phase. In other words, the net tritium current arising from dispersion terms in \cref{eq:CT2,eq:PT2} must be null at the tank's boundaries for the liquid phase. This is enforced by the Neumann BCs:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC3}
        \frac{\partial\CT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=0}=0
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BC4}
        \frac{\partial\CT}{\partial z}\bigg \rvert_{z=H}=0
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\Cref{eq:BC3} implies that no tritium is transported from the top of the liquid surface to the above head space gas. This assumption is discussed in section \ref{sec:sparging_assumptions}.

Ultimately, what we are interested in is the evolution of the tritium inventory in the molten salt $n_{T_2,l} \:\mathrm{[molT_2]}$, which can be obtained from the profiles computed by the 1D ARD model. 

\subsubsection{Definition and conventions}

The profiles solved by the 1D ARD model are inherently space dependent, and because of the hydrostatic pressure in the tank $P_l(z)=P_\mathrm{top}+\rho_l\,g\,(H-z)$, all the hydrodynamic parameters are also space dependent. It will be useful in the following to represent an arbitrary spatially varying quantity $\psi(z)$ by its average over a volume $\alpha$, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:def_average}
    \langle \psi \rangle^\alpha \equiv \frac{\iiint_{V_\alpha}\psi(\vec r) \,dV_\alpha}{V_\alpha}
\end{equation}

When $\alpha$ is the liquid or gas phase ($l,g$) it should be specified in the notation, but we set the convention to omit it when it refers to the whole system (i.e. the tank $t=l \cup g$). Defining $H_\alpha\equiv\frac{V_\alpha}{A}$ and considering that $\iiint_{V_\alpha}\psi(\vec r) \,dV_\alpha=A\,\int_{H_\alpha}\psi(z)\,dz_\alpha$ since we are in 1D, it follows that:
\begin{align}
    \langle \varepsilon_l \rangle &= \frac{\int_{H_t}\varepsilon_l(z) \,dz_t}{H_t}\\
    \langle \CT \rangle^l &= \frac{\int_{H_l}\CT(z) \,dz_l}{H_l} 
\end{align}

It comes naturally from the definition of the phase fraction (\cref{eq:def_eps}) that $\langle \varepsilon_l \rangle = \frac{V_l}{V_t}$. Similarly, the generic definition of the concentration (\cref{eq:def_c_generic}) and its average are consistent: $\langle \CT \rangle^l = \frac{n_{T_2.l}}{V_l}$.

\paragraph{Evolution of the tritium inventory}

\subsection{Closure relations resolution}

There are several available correlations in the literature for the same physical quantity, and the choice of which correlation to use is sometimes non-trivial: it changes depending on what parameters are already known and in what range they are. \todo{give an example (bubble size)}

In particular, the topic of gas sparging in molten salts presents large unknowns (\todo{add diffusivity/solubility correlation plots in Background and refer to it here}), and it is the purpose of this model to explore them. It was therefore designed to offer flexibility as to the closure relations used and the assumptions they are built upon. 

This flexibility is achieved by dynamically resolving the dependencies between all the parameters of the model every time a new simulation is launched, based on a set of closure relations and known parameters specified by the user for this specific simulation. Here, a \textit{parameters} can be a physical quantity (e.g. temperature) or more broadly any quantity used to describes the system (e.g. gas fraction, dimensionless numbers), it doesn't have to be a scalar, some parameters have a spatially varying profile (the pressure dependent ones); a \textit{closure relations} is any relation between parameters (e.g. correlation, state equation, definition).

Each closure relation that gives a parameter $p_j$ is a function $f_j$ of $n$ other parameters $p_i$ ($i=1,...,n\neq j$). The $p_i$ are either known (given by the user or already computed), or they have to be obtained as the output of an other closure relation $f_i$. The problem of dynamically resolving this potentially complex network of interdependent parameters can be described using the formalism of mathematical graphs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item each parameter $p_j$ is a \textit{node} $j$ of the graph;
    \item the closure relation $f_j$ that computes $p_j$ from other parameters $p_i$ gives the \textit{ingoing links}, from each node $i=1,...,n$ to node $j$;
    \item the closure relations $f_k(...,p_j,...)$ that require $p_j$ as an input give the \textit{outgoing links}, from node $j$ to each node $k$.
\end{itemize}   
An example of the typical dependencies of the sparging model is given in \todo{add dependencies diagram}, obtained using the closure relations presented in \cref{sec:sparging_maths}. Note that a node $j$ can be computed (i.e. is \textit{discovered}) only if the $n$ nodes it depends on have been discovered already. This prohibits cyclic dependencies, where a node needs itself to be computed, i.e. where $p_j=f_j(p_i,...)=f_j(f_i(p_j,...),...)$.

Eventually, what is required by the sparging model are the ARD parameters that appear in the 1D ARD equations. The intermediate parameters that were used to derive the latter are valuable to understand the model. The input parameters are considered to be independently controlled by the user in the actual sparging experiment, they are the "bedrock" level of the graph in the sense that they cannot be obtained from closure relations. Computing the ARD parameters boils down to discovering the above described oriented graph, it is achieved by the closure relations resolution stage by following this sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the user specifies a set of known parameters (nodes) $N=\{p_i\}$ and closure relations (links) $L=\{f_k\}$ The known parameters can be input, but also intermediate or ARD parameters if the user wants to short-circuit some closure relations;
    \item the graph search algorithm is given a list of nodes it has to discover $\hat N=\{p_k, k=\mathrm{ARD \ parameters}\}$;
    \item for each element of $\hat N$, it recursively goes upstream the ingoing links leading to the required node $k$, following the set of links given in $L$, until reaching a known node $i$, which it uses to compute all the nodes on the way back from $i$ to $k$;
    \item each node thereby computed is added to the list of discovered nodes $N$ until it includes all the required nodes, when $\hat N \subset N$
\end{enumerate}
The parameters resolution algorithm will fail if the user provides a set of known parameters and relations that is insufficient to compute the ARD parameters (\textit{incomplete}). 

The \verb|from_parameters()| method of the \verb|SimulationInput| class implements this graph discovery, the recursive part of the algorithm is summarized in \cref{fig:graph_search}.

\subsubsection{Derivation}
Let us consider a single He bubble $b$, immersed in a static liquid of tritium concentration $c_l$. The evolution of the tritium inventory $n_{T_2,b}$ in the bubble is governed by:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nb}
    \frac{d\,n_{T_2,b}}{dt} = A_b\,\JT
\end{equation}

Writing it in terms of tritium partial pressure by using the ideal gas law and expanding the right term, \cref{eq:nb} becomes:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pb_ODE}
    \frac{V_b}{RT}\frac{d\,\PT}{dt} = A_b\,h_l\,(c_l-K_s\PT)
\end{equation}

We are assuming a spherical bubble of diameter $d_b$, the specific interfacial area of the bubble is $a_b=\frac{A_b}{V_b}=\frac{6}{d_b}$ (not to be confused with the specific interfacial area of the gas phase as a whole $a=\varepsilon_ga_b$).
Solving \cref{eq:Pb_ODE} yields:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pb_full}
    \PT(t)=\frac{c_l}{K_s}\,(1-e^{-a_b\,R\,T\,h_l\,K_s\,t})
\end{equation}

The speed $v_b$ of the bubble, assumed constant, couples the time it spends in the system to its position: $t=\frac{z}{v_b}$. We can express the member of the exponential term in \cref{eq:Pb_full} as a dimensionless number $\Pi$ defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pi_def}
    \Pi(z)\equiv \frac{a_b\,R\,T\,h_l\,K_s}{v_g}\,z
\end{equation}

$\Pi$ can be interpreted as "how close the bubble is to reaching $\frac{c_l}{K_s}$, i.e. being at equilibrium with the surrounding liquid". 
\begin{itemize}
    \item When $1-e^{\Pi(z)}\approx0$: the bubble can be considered free of any tritium, the driving force for tritium exchange \JT is therefore maximal, limited only by the liquid side mass transfer $h_l$, i.e. the ability of tritium to diffuse down the concentration gradient, through the liquid film up to the gas phase. 
    \item When $1-e^{\Pi(z)}\approx1$: the bubble is saturated with tritium, the interfacial flux \JT drops as there is no concentration gradient through the liquid film to drive it, since the mass transfer process is throttled by the partial pressure in the bubble being at equilibrium with the liquid phase.
\end{itemize}
